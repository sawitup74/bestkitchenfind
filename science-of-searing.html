The Science of a Great Sear

Why browned food tastes so good, what actually creates a crust, and practical ways to get a deep, savory sear on steak, chicken, vegetables and fish.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

The crust on a great steak is one of the most delicious things in cooking. That deep brown, crunchy, savory layer is where most of the flavor lives. It is not a coincidence or a matter of luck; it is chemistry. Once you understand what is happening on the surface of your food when it hits hot steel, you can get a better sear on everything from ribeyes to scallops to cauliflower.

The Maillard reaction

When proteins and sugars are heated together, they react to form hundreds of new compounds that create the rich, roasted, savory flavors and brown color of seared food. This is known as the Maillard reaction, named after the French chemist who described it in the early 1900s.

The Maillard reaction is what makes toast taste different from bread, roasted coffee different from green beans, and a seared steak different from a boiled one. It happens slowly at moderate temperatures, but it speeds up dramatically once the surface of food reaches about 280 to 330°F.

Caramelization

Caramelization is a different but related reaction: the browning of sugars alone. It happens at higher temperatures, usually above about 320°F, and produces sweet, nutty, slightly bitter flavors. Onions, carrots and other vegetables with natural sugars caramelize beautifully on a hot grill.

The enemy of browning: water

Water boils at 212°F. As long as the surface of your food is wet, its temperature cannot rise much above that, and the Maillard reaction stays slow. All the energy from your fire goes into evaporating water instead of browning.

This is why wet steaks steam and turn gray, why crowded pans produce soggy vegetables, and why marinated meat sometimes struggles to brown.

The solution is simple: dry the surface.

How to get a better sear

1. Dry the food

Pat meat and fish thoroughly with paper towels right before cooking. For the best results, salt meat in advance and refrigerate it uncovered on a rack for several hours or overnight. This dry brining draws moisture out, then the salt dissolves in it and is reabsorbed, leaving a seasoned interior and a dry surface.

2. Use high, intense heat

The hotter the cooking surface, the faster moisture evaporates and the faster browning begins. On a charcoal grill, that means fully lit coals, piled close to the grate. On a griddle or cast-iron skillet, it means preheating for 10 to 15 minutes.

3. Choose a heavy cooking surface

Thick metal stores a lot of heat. When cold food hits a thin pan, the pan cools down and stops searing. When cold food hits thick carbon steel or cast iron, the metal holds its temperature. That is why heavy griddles and grates produce better crusts. Thick steel grates also leave darker, deeper sear marks.

4. Do not crowd

Food placed too close together traps steam. Give each piece some space so moisture can escape.

5. Do not move it too soon

Food that is browning releases naturally from the cooking surface. If you try to move it too early, it sticks and tears. Wait until it releases, then flip.

6. Use the right fat

A thin layer of oil helps conduct heat into the uneven surface of food, improving browning. Use a high smoke point oil like avocado, canola, peanut or refined sunflower. Butter browns and adds flavor, but its milk solids burn above about 350°F. Add butter at the end of cooking for basting.

7. Add a little sugar or protein

Small amounts of sugar or protein in a marinade or rub speed up browning. Honey, brown sugar, soy sauce, mayonnaise and yogurt all promote a darker crust. Use sugar sparingly, since it burns quickly.

8. Consider baking soda

A pinch of baking soda raises the pH of food's surface, which accelerates the Maillard reaction. A little baking powder mixed into the salt you rub on chicken skin dramatically improves crisping. Too much tastes soapy, so use only small amounts.

Grill marks versus all-over crust

Grill marks look appealing, but they represent only a fraction of the surface area. The spaces between the marks stay comparatively pale. A full crust across the whole surface delivers more flavor.

For more crust, flip food more frequently over a hot fire, or use a griddle or cast-iron skillet set on the grate, which contacts the entire surface. Many cooks combine both: start on the grate for marks and smoke, then finish on the griddle for total crust.

Flipping frequently

Old advice said to flip a steak only once. But studies by food scientists have shown that flipping every 30 seconds to a minute can actually cook a steak faster and more evenly, because each side alternately heats and cools, reducing the overcooked gray band beneath the crust. The crust develops a little more slowly, but more evenly. Try both methods and see which you prefer.

Searing specific foods

Steak

Dry brine overnight. Pat dry. Cook over high heat, or reverse sear for thick cuts. Sear 1 to 2 minutes per side for a thick crust.

Chicken

Dry brine with salt and baking powder. Render the skin over indirect heat, then crisp over direct heat.

Pork chops

Brine or dry brine. Sear over high heat for 2 to 3 minutes per side, then move to indirect heat to finish.

Fish

Dry thoroughly and refrigerate uncovered for 30 minutes. Use a very hot, clean, oiled grate or griddle. Do not move until it releases.

Scallops

Buy "dry" scallops, which have not been treated with a preserving solution. Pat dry, sear on a very hot, oiled griddle for 90 seconds per side.

Vegetables

Cut to maximize flat surfaces. Toss lightly with oil and salt. Cook on high heat in a single layer.

Tofu

Press out moisture with a heavy weight wrapped in a towel for 20 minutes. Cut into slabs, oil, and sear on a hot griddle.

Signs of a perfect sear

  • Deep brown color, not black.
  • A crisp, slightly crunchy texture.
  • A rich, nutty, roasted aroma.
  • Food releases easily from the cooking surface.

When browning goes too far

Charring can be delicious in small amounts, but heavily blackened, burnt food tastes bitter. Keep an eye on sugary marinades and thin foods, and use a two-zone fire so you can move food away from the heat.

The takeaway

A great sear depends on dry food, intense heat, a heavy cooking surface and a little patience. Combine those factors and you will get the deep, savory crust that makes grilled food irresistible. Every time you cook, you are running a small chemistry experiment. Now you know how to make it work in your favor.

Going further: practical searing tips

Dry surface, hot steel

Moisture is the enemy of a great sear. Water has to evaporate before browning can begin, so a wet steak steams first. Pat meat thoroughly dry, and if you dry brine, leave it uncovered in the refrigerator so the surface dries.

Fat choice

Use a high-smoke-point fat for searing on a griddle or skillet, such as avocado oil or refined peanut oil. Save butter for basting at the end, after the heat comes down.

Don't crowd

Too much food on a griddle drops the temperature and causes steaming. Leave space between pieces.

Let it release

Meat sticks at first, then releases when a crust forms. If you have to tug, wait a little longer.

Sear first or last?

Thin cuts are best seared hot and fast from start to finish. Thick cuts often benefit from a reverse sear: gentle heat first, then a hard sear at the end, which gives a thinner gray band and a more even center.

Rest briefly

A short rest lets juices settle a little, but a long rest softens the crust. For seared steaks, rest about 5 minutes.

Quick questions

What creates a seared crust?

The Maillard reaction between proteins and sugars, which accelerates when the surface of food is dry and very hot.

Why does my steak turn gray instead of brown?

The surface was wet, the heat too low, or the food was crowded. Moisture keeps the surface near 212°F.

Should I flip steak once or often?

Either works. Frequent flipping cooks more evenly; flipping once can build a faster crust.
