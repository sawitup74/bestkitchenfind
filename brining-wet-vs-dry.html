Brining Explained: Wet Brine vs. Dry Brine for Juicier Meat

How salt makes meat juicier, the exact ratios and times for chicken, pork, turkey and steak, and when to choose wet or dry.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

If you have ever bitten into a grilled pork chop or chicken breast that was dry and bland, brining is the cure. Brining uses salt to season meat deeply and help it hold onto moisture during cooking. There are two main methods: wet brining, which soaks meat in salt water, and dry brining, which coats meat with salt and lets it rest. Both work, and each has advantages.

How brining works

When salt comes into contact with meat, it first draws moisture out. Then that moisture dissolves the salt, forming a concentrated brine that the meat reabsorbs. Over time, salt moves deep into the muscle and changes the structure of proteins, so they hold more water and squeeze out less during cooking. The result is juicier, more evenly seasoned meat.

Wet brining

How it works

Meat is submerged in a solution of salt and water, often with sugar and aromatics, for a period of time. It absorbs some water along with the salt.

Basic wet brine ratio

A standard brine is about 1/4 cup of light, flaky kosher salt per quart of water, or about 3 tablespoons of table salt per quart. Different salts have different densities, so weigh salt when possible: about 5 to 6 percent salt by weight for short brines, less for longer brines.

Optional additions

  • Sugar (brown or white) for browning and balance
  • Garlic, peppercorns, bay leaves
  • Herbs such as rosemary and thyme
  • Citrus peels
  • Apple cider, beer or tea instead of some water

Wet brining pros and cons

Pros:

  • Very juicy results, especially for lean meats.
  • Fast for small cuts.
  • Easy to add aromatics.

Cons:

  • Takes up a lot of refrigerator space.
  • Can dilute flavor slightly with water.
  • Can make skin less crisp.
  • Over-brining can produce a spongy or ham-like texture.

Dry brining

How it works

Salt is sprinkled directly on the meat, which then rests uncovered in the refrigerator. The salt draws out moisture, dissolves, and is reabsorbed. The surface dries out, which helps create a crisp crust or skin.

Basic dry brine ratio

About 3/4 teaspoon of light, flaky kosher salt per pound of meat, or about 1/2 teaspoon of table salt or a dense, fine-grained kosher salt per pound.

Dry brining times

FoodTime
Steaks45 minutes or 1 to 2 days
Pork chops4 hours to 1 day
Chicken pieces4 hours to 1 day
Whole chicken1 to 2 days
Turkey1 to 3 days
Roasts1 to 3 days

Note: if you cannot dry brine for at least 40 minutes, salt right before cooking instead. In the in-between period, the salt has drawn moisture to the surface but it has not been reabsorbed.

Dry brining pros and cons

Pros:

  • Concentrated flavor.
  • Crisp skin and great crust.
  • No space-hogging containers.
  • Simple.

Cons:

  • Takes longer than wet brining.
  • Slightly less juicy than a wet brine for very lean meats.

For most grilling, dry brining is the simpler and more versatile choice.

Step-by-step dry brine for grilled chicken

  1. Pat chicken pieces dry.
  2. Sprinkle with 3/4 teaspoon light, flaky kosher salt per pound on all sides.
  3. Place on a rack set over a sheet pan.
  4. Refrigerate uncovered for 4 to 24 hours.
  5. Do not rinse. Pat dry if needed.
  6. Season with salt-free rub or pepper, and grill over a two-zone fire.

Step-by-step wet brine for pork chops

  1. Dissolve 1/4 cup light, flaky kosher salt and 2 tablespoons brown sugar in 1 quart warm water. Add garlic, peppercorns and a bay leaf.
  2. Cool the brine completely.
  3. Submerge pork chops and refrigerate for 2 to 4 hours.
  4. Remove, rinse briefly if desired, and pat very dry.
  5. Grill to 145°F, then rest for 3 minutes.

Brining safety

  • Always brine in the refrigerator.
  • Cool hot brines before adding meat.
  • Discard used brine.
  • Use a food-safe container.
  • Do not brine meat that has already been salted or is labeled "enhanced," "basted" or "kosher" (kosher meat has already been salted during processing).

Common mistakes

  • Using the wrong salt amount. Salts vary in density. Use the right ratio for your salt.
  • Over-brining. Too long in a wet brine can make texture spongy.
  • Adding more salt later. Brined meat is already seasoned. Taste before adding more.
  • Not drying the surface. Wet meat steams instead of searing.

The takeaway

Brining is one of the simplest ways to improve grilled meat. Dry brining gives concentrated flavor and crisp skin with almost no effort; wet brining gives extra juiciness to lean meats. Choose the method that fits your schedule and the cut, and check doneness with a thermometer for perfect results.

Going further: salt, science and special cases

Weighing salt is the cure for confusion

Two teaspoons of one brand of kosher salt can weigh nearly twice as much as two teaspoons of another, because the crystals are shaped differently. If your recipes keep coming out too salty or too bland, weigh salt with a kitchen scale. For dry brining, a good target is about 1 percent of the meat's weight in salt. For a 2-pound chicken, that is 20 grams. For wet brines, about 5 percent salt by weight of the water is a strong short brine, while 3 percent works for long soaks.

Equilibrium brines

An equilibrium brine uses just enough salt that the meat will never become too salty, no matter how long it soaks. Weigh the meat and water together and add salt equal to about 1 to 1.5 percent of the combined weight. Because the meat can only absorb salt until it matches the brine, you can leave it longer without risk of over-salting. This is a handy method if your schedule is unpredictable.

Brining in the cooler

For big cooks, like several whole chickens or a turkey, refrigerator space runs out quickly. A clean cooler works: use a food-safe brining bag, add the brine, then pack ice around the bag. Keep the temperature at 40°F or below and check it with your thermometer. Add ice as needed.

Brining and smoke

Brined meat browns more quickly because of salt and any added sugar. When grilling over charcoal, start brined poultry over the coal-free half of a two-zone fire and finish over the coals, watching closely. Sugary brines especially can scorch.

Brined shrimp on the griddle

A 15-minute brine of 1 tablespoon salt and 1 teaspoon baking soda per quart of water gives shrimp a plump, snappy texture. Rinse, pat very dry and sear on a hot griddle with oil, garlic and chili flakes.

Skin tricks for crispy poultry

For the crispiest skin on grilled chicken, add a little baking powder (not baking soda) to the dry brine: about 1 teaspoon per pound, mixed with the salt. It raises the skin's pH, helping it brown and crisp. Leave the chicken uncovered on a rack in the refrigerator overnight to dry the surface.

What not to brine

  • Very fatty cuts, like a well-marbled ribeye, benefit more from a simple dry brine than a wet one.
  • Ground meat should not be brined; salt changes its texture to sausage-like. Salt burgers only on the outside just before cooking.
  • Pre-seasoned or "enhanced" products are already salty.

A brining schedule for the weekend

  • Friday night: dry brine steaks and chicken.
  • Saturday morning: wet brine pork chops for a midday cook.
  • Saturday afternoon: brine shrimp just before grilling.

Cook's notebook

Bottom line

When in doubt, dry brine overnight. It is the simplest method with the biggest payoff.

Quick questions

Should I rinse meat after brining?

After a dry brine, no. After a wet brine, a quick rinse is optional; always pat dry.

Can I dry brine overnight?

Yes. For steaks, chicken and pork, an overnight dry brine in the refrigerator works very well.

Should I brine kosher or enhanced meat?

No. Those are already salted during processing.
