Carbon Steel Care

How to Clean a Carbon Steel Grill After Every Cook

A five-minute post-cook routine for grates, griddles and fireboxes, plus deep cleaning, what never to do, and how to handle stuck-on messes.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

Cleaning a grill is the least glamorous part of cooking outdoors, and also one of the most important. A clean grate releases food better, tastes better and lasts longer. Carbon steel in particular rewards good habits: a few minutes of care after each cook keeps its seasoning strong and prevents rust. Ignore it, and you will be fighting sticking, off flavors and orange spots.

The good news is that cleaning carbon steel is quick and easy once you have a routine.

The golden rule: clean while warm

Food residue is much easier to remove from warm steel. Grease is still liquid, burnt bits are crisp, and stuck-on sugars are soft. Once the grill cools, everything hardens and sticks.

Plan to clean right after cooking, while the coals are dying and the steel is still warm but not dangerously hot.

Tools you need

  • A stiff grill brush or scraper. Brushes with tightly wound bristles or bristle-free scrapers work well. Inspect any wire brush regularly and replace it if bristles loosen; stray wires can end up in food.
  • A flat metal spatula or bench scraper for griddles.
  • Paper towels or rags.
  • Tongs to hold paper towels.
  • A squeeze bottle of water for stubborn griddle spots.
  • Oil or seasoning balm.
  • Heat-resistant gloves.

The five-minute routine for grates

  1. Scrape. While the grate is still warm, scrub it with a brush or scraper to remove food bits and carbon.
  2. Wipe. Fold a paper towel, hold it with tongs and wipe the grate to remove loosened debris and ash.
  3. Oil. Dip a clean paper towel in a little oil or apply a small amount of seasoning balm, and wipe a very thin layer over the grate.
  4. Heat briefly. If there is still heat in the coals, let the oiled grate sit over them for a few minutes to bond the oil. If not, just let it cool.

That is it. Doing this after every cook keeps the grate seasoned and ready.

The five-minute routine for griddles

  1. Scrape grease and food debris toward the edge with a flat spatula or bench scraper. Push it off into a catch tray or onto the coals.
  2. Steam stubborn spots. Squirt a little water on stuck-on bits while the griddle is hot. It will sizzle and lift the residue. Scrape again.
  3. Wipe with paper towels until clean.
  4. Oil with a very thin layer.

Avoid pouring large amounts of cold water on a very hot griddle. Rapid temperature changes can warp thin metal; thick steel handles it far better, but small amounts of water are still the smart approach.

Cleaning the firebox

After the coals are fully out and the grill is completely cool:

  1. Remove the grates.
  2. Dump the cold ash into a metal container.
  3. Brush out remaining ash with a dry brush.
  4. Check for grease buildup on the walls and scrape it off.
  5. If the steel is dry and dull, wipe on a thin coat of seasoning.

Never leave ash sitting in a grill. Ash absorbs moisture from the air, and wet ash is corrosive to steel.

Deep cleaning

Once or twice a season, or if your grill has been sitting for a while, do a deeper clean.

  1. Disassemble the grill if possible. Flat-pack grills that come apart into individual plates make this much easier.
  2. Scrub every piece with hot, soapy water and a stiff brush or non-scratch pad.
  3. For heavy carbon buildup, use a metal scraper or steel wool. You may remove some seasoning; that is fine.
  4. Rinse and dry immediately and completely.
  5. Heat each piece briefly to drive off moisture.
  6. Re-season with a few thin coats.

Is soap okay?

Yes, in moderation. The old idea that soap destroys seasoning comes from a time when soaps contained lye. Modern dish soap is mild and will not harm well-polymerized seasoning. What does damage seasoning is soaking, abrasive scrubbing and prolonged contact with water. Wash quickly, rinse and dry immediately.

What never to do

  • Do not soak steel in water. It will rust.
  • Do not put carbon steel in a dishwasher. The heat, detergents and long exposure to water will strip seasoning and cause rust.
  • Do not leave it wet. Dry immediately and completely.
  • Do not use oven cleaner on cooking surfaces. It strips seasoning and is not food-safe.
  • Do not leave ash in the firebox. It holds moisture and causes corrosion.
  • Do not store it dirty. Grease can go rancid and attract pests.

Handling specific messes

Sugary glaze or barbecue sauce

Sugar burns into a hard, sticky crust. While still warm, scrape with a metal scraper. Steam with a little water if needed. Then re-oil.

Melted cheese

Let it crisp on a hot griddle, then scrape it off. A little water helps.

Burnt-on fish skin

Heat the grate over coals to carbonize the residue, then scrape.

Rancid oil smell

If a grill has been stored with too much oil, it may smell stale. Scrub with hot soapy water, dry, heat and re-season with thinner coats.

White or gray dull spots

Usually ash residue or acid that has etched the seasoning. Clean, dry and re-season the area.

Cleaning the thermometer and tools

Wash tongs, spatulas and thermometer probes with hot, soapy water after each cook. Many digital thermometers, including the Slot Grill Instant-Read Thermometer, are water-resistant but should be hand washed and not submerged.

Wire brush safety

Wire bristles can break off grill brushes and stick to grates, then end up in food. To reduce the risk:

  • Inspect brushes before each use.
  • Replace worn brushes.
  • Wipe the grate with a damp paper towel after brushing.
  • Consider bristle-free scrapers, coil brushes or a wad of foil held with tongs.

The takeaway

The best way to keep carbon steel looking and cooking great is a simple habit: scrape, wipe and oil after every cook, and empty the ash once cool. Take five minutes at the end of each grilling session and your grill will be ready to go, rust-free and non-stick, every time you light it.

Going further: a cleaning routine by frequency

After every cook

While the steel is still warm, but not hot enough to burn you, scrape the grates with a flat scraper or a ball of foil held in tongs. Dump cooled ash into a metal can. Wipe the grates with a paper towel dipped in a little high-smoke-point oil, held in tongs. That thirty-second habit keeps rust away and builds seasoning with every fire.

Every few cooks

Look for flaking black bits on the grates and griddle. Those are carbonized food and loose old seasoning, not paint. Scrape them off, then re-oil and heat. Check the inside walls of the firebox for buildup, and brush away loose soot.

Once or twice a season

Do a deeper clean: scrub every piece with hot water and a stiff brush, dry each piece immediately over heat or with towels, and apply a thin coat of seasoning balm to the whole grill, then heat to set it. The balm works on the grill body as a food-safe alternative to barbecue paint.

What to avoid

  • Soaking carbon steel in water overnight.
  • Running it through a dishwasher.
  • Harsh oven cleaners on cooking surfaces.
  • Leaving acidic sauces sitting on the steel.
  • Storing it wet or in a sealed bin while damp.

When soap is fine

A little dish soap will not ruin a well-built seasoning layer. Use it when you need to remove heavy grease, then rinse, dry right away and re-oil. The real enemies of carbon steel are standing water and long soaks, not a quick wash.

Cleaning the griddle plate

Scrape while warm, then squirt a little water onto the hot plate to lift stuck bits; it will steam vigorously, so keep your hands back. Scrape again, wipe dry and apply a thin film of oil.

Quick questions

Is it okay to use soap on carbon steel?

Yes, briefly. Modern dish soap won't harm good seasoning. Avoid soaking, and dry immediately.

When should I clean my grill?

Right after cooking, while the steel is still warm. Residue comes off much more easily.

Why should I empty the ash?

Ash absorbs moisture and becomes corrosive, which can rust a steel firebox.
