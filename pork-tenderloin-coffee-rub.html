Coffee-Rubbed Pork Tenderloin

A dark, savory crust and a rosy, tender center in about 25 minutes: the weeknight roast that tastes like a weekend project.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

Pork tenderloin is the most underrated weeknight protein around. It is lean, tender, inexpensive, and it cooks in about the time it takes to boil pasta. On a charcoal grill it develops a smoky, caramelized crust that turns a humble cut into something special. This recipe uses a coffee and chili rub that builds a deep, almost mahogany bark and pairs with a quick peach and mustard glaze.

Getting to know the cut

The tenderloin is a long, thin muscle that runs along the spine. It does very little work, which makes it extremely tender and very lean. Tenderloins usually weigh 1 to 1.5 pounds each and are often sold two to a package. One tenderloin feeds two to three people.

Do not confuse it with pork loin, a much larger, wider roast that cooks very differently.

Most tenderloins have a thin, silvery membrane called silverskin along one side. It does not melt during cooking and becomes chewy, so trim it off. Slide the tip of a thin knife under one end of the membrane, angle the blade slightly upward, and slice along the length while pulling the silverskin taut with your other hand.

The coffee rub

Coffee might sound odd on pork, but finely ground coffee adds a deep, roasty, slightly bitter note that balances sugar and spice. It does not taste like a cup of coffee; it tastes like savory depth.

For two tenderloins:

  • 2 tablespoons finely ground dark roast coffee
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon smoked paprika
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon ancho chili powder
  • 1 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1 teaspoon onion powder
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1/4 teaspoon cayenne

Mix and store extra in a jar; it keeps for months and is also great on steak and ribs.

Prep

Trim the silverskin and pat the tenderloins dry. Fold the skinny tail end under itself and tie it with butcher twine so the tenderloin has an even thickness from end to end. This simple step keeps the thin end from overcooking.

Rub each tenderloin with a teaspoon of oil, then press the rub firmly onto every surface. Use it all; this is a lean cut that can handle a thick coat. Let it sit at room temperature for 20 to 30 minutes while you light the fire, or refrigerate up to 24 hours.

The fire

Set up the coals in two zones. Light a chimney of charcoal about three-quarters full. When the coals are ashed over, pour them onto one side of the grill and let the grate heat for five minutes. Brush and oil it.

On the Slot Grill Compact, one or two tenderloins fit lengthwise on the 16.25-inch grate. On the Full Size you can fit up to four, plus a few peach halves.

Grilling

  1. Sear the tenderloins over direct heat, turning a quarter turn every 2 minutes, until all sides are dark and crusty, about 8 minutes. Because of the sugar and coffee, the crust will look very dark. Watch for real burning; a deep brown-black is good, ashy gray is bad.
  2. Move them to the cool side, with the thickest end facing the coals.
  3. Cook for another 6 to 12 minutes, turning once, until the center of the thickest section reads 140 to 145°F.
  4. Transfer to a cutting board and rest for 5 to 10 minutes. The temperature will rise a few degrees.

USDA recommends cooking whole cuts of pork to 145°F with a three-minute rest. At that temperature the center will be blush pink and very juicy. Pork tenderloin cooked to 160°F is dry and grainy, so a thermometer is truly essential.

Peach-mustard glaze

While the pork cooks, make this simple glaze:

  • 1/3 cup peach preserves
  • 2 tablespoons whole-grain mustard
  • 1 tablespoon apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon bourbon (optional)
  • Pinch of salt and red pepper flakes

Warm everything in a small pan on the cool side of the grill or on the stove until melted and combined.

Brush the glaze over the tenderloins during the last 3 minutes of cooking on the cool side. Brush again after slicing.

Grilled peaches

If peaches are in season, grill them alongside. Halve and pit firm-ripe peaches, brush the cut sides with oil, and grill cut-side down over direct heat for 3 to 4 minutes, until marked and softened. Slice and serve with the pork.

Slicing and serving

Remove the twine, then slice the tenderloin on a slight angle into 1/2-inch medallions. Arrange on a platter with the grilled peaches and spoon the remaining glaze over the top. Scatter with chopped fresh thyme or parsley.

Great side dishes include:

  • Charred green beans with almonds and lemon
  • Creamy polenta or mashed sweet potatoes
  • Cornbread baked in a cast-iron skillet
  • A tangy cabbage slaw with apple and celery seed
  • Grilled corn with honey butter

Variations

Herb and garlic: rub with olive oil, minced garlic, rosemary, thyme, lemon zest, salt and pepper. Skip the glaze and serve with salsa verde.

Teriyaki: marinate in soy sauce, mirin, ginger, garlic and a little brown sugar for 2 to 4 hours. Grill the same way and brush with reduced marinade at the end.

Al pastor style: marinate in a blend of dried guajillo chilies, pineapple juice, vinegar, achiote, garlic and oregano. Slice thin and serve in tortillas with grilled pineapple.

Mustard and maple: brush with Dijon mustard, then press on a mix of cracked pepper and coarse salt. Glaze with maple syrup at the end.

Leftovers

Leftover tenderloin is incredibly versatile. Slice thin for sandwiches with mustard and pickles, chop it into fried rice, or toss with greens, grilled peaches, blue cheese and toasted pecans for a hearty salad. Refrigerated leftovers keep three to four days.

Troubleshooting

The crust burned before the inside was done. The direct heat was too hot or the tenderloin sat too long without turning. Turn more frequently and move to indirect heat sooner.

It is dry. It was overcooked. Pull at 140 to 145°F and rest.

It cooked unevenly. The tail end was not folded under. Tie it to create an even thickness.

The rub fell off. The surface was wet or the rub was not pressed in. Pat dry, use a light coat of oil and press firmly.

Why charcoal makes the difference

Tenderloin is mild, so it benefits enormously from the flavor of a live fire. The smoke from charcoal adds complexity that an oven cannot replicate, and the intense radiant heat builds a crust much faster than a pan. Because the cook is so short, you need a grill that heats up quickly and holds direct heat well. A sturdy steel charcoal grill does exactly that.

The takeaway

Trim, tie, rub, sear and finish gently. Pork tenderloin is proof that weeknight grilling can be fast, easy and seriously impressive. Make a double batch of the rub and keep it next to the stove; you will reach for it again and again.

Weeknight game plan

Because the cook is so short, the real secret to a weeknight tenderloin is having the rub already made. Mix a triple batch on Sunday and store it in a jar. On a weeknight, light the chimney the moment you walk in the door, trim and rub the pork while the coals catch, and set a pot of water to boil for a side of potatoes or rice. Twenty minutes after the coals hit the grill, you are slicing.

Here is a realistic timeline for a Tuesday dinner:

  1. 6:00: light a three-quarter chimney of charcoal.
  2. 6:05: trim, tie and rub the tenderloins; start a side dish indoors.
  3. 6:20: pour coals, heat and clean the grate.
  4. 6:25: sear the pork over direct heat.
  5. 6:33: move to the cool side, make the glaze.
  6. 6:43: check temperature and glaze.
  7. 6:45: rest, slice and eat by 6:55.

Wine and drinks

Pork with coffee and chili wants a fruity red with soft tannins. A chilled light red or a fruity rosé is lovely on a summer evening. If you prefer beer, an amber ale or a brown ale echoes the roasty flavors of the rub.

Quick questions

What temperature is pork tenderloin done?

USDA recommends 145°F followed by a three-minute rest. The center will be slightly pink and juicy.

Does coffee rub taste like coffee?

Not strongly. It adds roasty, savory depth and helps build a dark crust.

Why tie a pork tenderloin?

Folding and tying the thin tail end creates an even thickness so the whole roast cooks at the same rate.
