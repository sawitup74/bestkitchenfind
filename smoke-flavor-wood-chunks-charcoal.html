Adding Wood Smoke to a Charcoal Grill

How to use hardwood chunks to layer real smoke flavor into steaks, chicken, pork and vegetables, which woods to pick, and how much is too much.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

Charcoal on its own produces a clean, subtle flavor. That is a feature, not a bug: it lets the taste of the food and the browning take center stage. But sometimes you want more. A handful of hardwood on the coals can add sweet, aromatic, complex smoke to anything you grill. It is easy to do, it costs very little, and it can transform everyday cooks. It is also easy to overdo, so a little knowledge goes a long way.

How smoke flavors food

When wood burns, it releases compounds that settle on the surface of food. Some of them taste sweet and fruity; others are savory, spicy or bacon-like. The best smoke is thin and almost invisible, sometimes described as "blue smoke." Thick, billowing white smoke comes from wood that is smoldering without enough oxygen, and it tastes acrid and bitter.

Smoke sticks best to cool, moist surfaces. That is why food absorbs most of its smoke flavor early in the cook, while it is still cold.

Chunks, chips and logs

  • Chunks: fist-sized pieces of hardwood. They smolder steadily and last a long time. Best choice for charcoal grills.
  • Chips: small, thin pieces. They catch fire quickly and burn up in minutes. Better for short cooks or in a foil pouch.
  • Logs or splits: used as the main fuel in large offset smokers. Too big for most charcoal grills.
  • Pellets: compressed sawdust designed for pellet grills, though they can be used in a smoker tube.

Soak or not?

Many people soak wood chips or chunks in water before using them. The idea is to slow their burning. In reality, water does not penetrate very far into wood, and wet wood steams and produces thick, dirty smoke until it dries. For chunks, skip the soaking. For chips, you can soak them for 30 minutes if you want them to last a little longer, but dry chips in a foil pouch with a few holes poked in it work just as well.

Choosing your wood

Different woods produce different flavors. Use seasoned, dry hardwood from fruit and nut trees or traditional hardwoods. Never use softwoods like pine, fir, spruce or cedar (except as a cooking plank), or any treated, painted or stained wood.

How much wood to use

Less than you think. For most cooks on a charcoal grill:

  • Short cooks (under 30 minutes): one small chunk or a handful of chips.
  • Medium cooks (30 to 60 minutes): one or two fist-sized chunks.
  • Long cooks (over an hour): two or three chunks, added gradually.

Too much smoke makes food bitter and acrid. Start with less and add more next time if you want a stronger flavor.

Step by step

  1. Light a chimney of charcoal and set up a two-zone fire.
  2. Place one or two wood chunks directly on the hot coals, toward the edge of the pile so they smolder rather than burst into flame.
  3. Wait 5 to 10 minutes until the initial thick white smoke settles into a thin, wispy, bluish smoke.
  4. Put the food on the cool side, near the coals and wood.
  5. Add another chunk as the first one burns down, if desired.

On an open grill, smoke rises straight up and around the food rather than circulating as it does in a closed smoker. You will get a lighter smoke flavor, which is ideal for quick cooks like steaks, chicken pieces, chops and vegetables. For a stronger effect, tent the food on the cool side with a disposable foil pan to trap more smoke around it.

What to smoke on an everyday grill

Chicken thighs or wings

Apple, cherry or pecan. Cook on the cool side for 20 to 30 minutes with smoke, then crisp over direct heat.

Pork chops and tenderloin

Hickory and apple. Smoke on the cool side, then sear.

Steak

Oak or a small piece of mesquite. Use smoke during the slow phase of a reverse sear.

Salmon

Alder or apple. Cook skin-side down on the cool side, or on a plank.

Vegetables

Apple, maple or cherry. Smoke peppers, onions, tomatoes and mushrooms for salsas and sauces. Smoky charred tomatoes make an incredible salsa.

Cheese and nuts

Cold smoking takes special equipment, but you can lightly smoke nuts on a sheet pan on the cool side for 20 minutes with a small chunk of fruitwood.

Corn

Smoked corn on the cob has a sweet, campfire flavor. Grill in the husk on the cool side with a chunk of cherry.

Smoke and safety

  • Grill only outdoors, in a well-ventilated area.
  • Keep a bucket of water or sand nearby, especially when using wood, since it can flare.
  • Use only clean, natural wood.
  • Let all coals and wood burn out or extinguish them completely before disposing of them.

Common mistakes

Too much wood. The food tastes bitter and harsh. Use one or two chunks for most cooks.

Starting too soon. Putting food on during the thick white smoke phase coats it in bitter flavors. Wait for thin smoke.

Wet wood. It steams and smolders. Use dry, seasoned chunks.

Wrong wood. Softwoods contain resins that taste terrible and can coat food in soot.

Expecting smoker results. An open grill gives a lighter, subtler smoke. That is perfect for everyday cooking.

Storing wood

Keep wood chunks dry, off the ground, and away from insects. A lidded plastic bin works well. Most bags of wood chunks keep for years if dry.

Smoke flavor without wood

You can add smoky notes to food in other ways:

  • Smoked paprika or chipotle in rubs
  • Smoked salt
  • A little liquid smoke in a marinade (use sparingly)
  • Charring vegetables over high heat

The takeaway

A chunk or two of the right hardwood can turn simple grilled food into something extraordinary. Start with mild fruitwoods, use a light hand, wait for thin smoke, and let the flavor build. Smoke is a seasoning, not the main ingredient.

Going further: getting clean smoke on an open grill

Thin blue smoke vs. white smoke

The smoke you want is thin and pale, almost blue. Thick white billowing smoke usually means the wood is smoldering without enough oxygen and will make food taste acrid and bitter. Let wood chunks catch fully and burn cleanly before putting food over them, and keep airflow open.

Placing wood on a two-zone fire

Set one or two fist-sized chunks directly on the hot coals, at the edge closest to the food on the cool side. That way the smoke drifts across the food as it cooks indirectly. Add one more chunk only when the first is mostly burned.

Less is more

On an open grill, smoke flavor builds quickly on thin foods and is easy to overdo. Start with one chunk. You can always add more next time.

Wood safety

Use only clean, untreated hardwood meant for cooking. Never burn pressure-treated lumber, painted wood, plywood or pallets, which can release harmful chemicals.

Cook's notebook

Chips vs. chunks

Small chips burn quickly on hot coals. Chunks smolder longer and give steadier smoke, so they suit open grills better.

Quick questions

Should I soak wood chunks?

No. Water barely penetrates the wood and only causes steamy, dirty smoke. Use dry chunks.

How much wood should I add to a charcoal grill?

One or two fist-sized chunks for most cooks under an hour. Start small.

Which wood is best for beginners?

Apple or cherry. They are mild and sweet and pair with almost anything.
