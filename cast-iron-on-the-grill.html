Cooking With Cast Iron on a Charcoal Grill

Skillets, Dutch ovens and griddles over live fire: what to cook, how to manage heat, and how to keep heavy pans safe and stable.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

Cast iron and charcoal belong together. A heavy skillet set on a grate over hot coals is one of the most versatile cooking setups in the world. It lets you sear steaks with butter, fry eggs and bacon, bake cornbread, simmer stews and roast vegetables, all with a subtle kiss of smoke you cannot get indoors. It also keeps the smoke and grease spatter of high-heat cooking out of your kitchen.

Why cook in cast iron on a grill?

  • Total contact searing. A skillet makes full contact with the surface of food, giving a deep, all-over crust rather than grill marks.
  • Cooking small or delicate foods. Shrimp, sliced vegetables, eggs and flaky fish will not fall through a pan.
  • Liquids and sauces. You can braise, simmer, baste and make pan sauces.
  • Baking. Cornbread, cobblers, skillet cookies and even pizza.
  • Keeping smoke outside. Searing steak in a hot skillet indoors sets off smoke alarms. Outside, nobody cares.

Is your grill strong enough?

Cast iron is heavy. A 12-inch skillet weighs about 8 pounds empty. A 6-quart Dutch oven full of stew can top 15 pounds. Thin, stamped grills and flimsy grates can bend, sag or tip under that weight, especially when they are hot.

Before you put cast iron on a grill, make sure the frame and grate are sturdy and the grill sits level on a stable, non-flammable surface. Thick steel grills are ideal. Choosing pans

  • 10- or 12-inch skillet: the most versatile. Sear steaks, fry potatoes, bake cornbread.
  • Cast-iron griddle or flat plate: pancakes, smash burgers, quesadillas.
  • Dutch oven (5 to 7 quarts): chili, stews, beans, braises, baking.
  • Grill pan: ridged cast iron for grill marks on delicate items.
  • Small skillets: individual desserts, dips and side dishes.

Pans without wooden or plastic handles are a must; those parts will melt or burn.

Fire setup

Use a two-zone fire. Cast iron takes time to heat, but once hot it holds heat very well. You will often want to start a pan directly over the coals to heat it, then move it to the cooler side to control cooking.

For searing, put the skillet right over a full, hot bed of coals and preheat for at least 10 minutes. For simmering, use fewer coals or move the pot to the edge of the fire. For baking, use indirect heat with a cover.

Recipes that shine

Butter-basted steak

Heat a skillet over hot coals until smoking. Add a thin film of oil and a dry-brined steak. Sear for 2 minutes, flip and sear another 2 minutes. Add 3 tablespoons butter, 3 smashed garlic cloves and a few sprigs of thyme. Tilt the pan and spoon the foaming butter over the steak repeatedly for 1 to 2 minutes. Pull at your target temperature and rest.

Crispy smashed potatoes

Boil small potatoes until tender. Smash each flat. Heat oil in a skillet over medium-high heat and fry the potatoes until golden and crisp on both sides. Toss with garlic, herbs and flaky salt.

Skillet shrimp with garlic and chili

Heat olive oil in a skillet. Add sliced garlic and chili flakes, cook until fragrant, then add shrimp. Cook 1 to 2 minutes per side. Finish with lemon juice, butter and parsley. Serve with grilled bread.

Blistered green beans

Toss green beans with oil and salt and char in a very hot skillet, stirring occasionally, until blistered. Add soy sauce, garlic and chili crisp.

Fajitas

Sear strips of marinated skirt steak or chicken in a ripping-hot skillet with sliced peppers and onions. Serve sizzling with warm tortillas.

Skillet cobbler

Toss sliced peaches or berries with sugar, lemon and a little cornstarch in a skillet. Top with biscuit dough. Bake over indirect heat, covered with foil, for 35 to 45 minutes until bubbly.

Skillet cookie

Press a batch of chocolate chip cookie dough into a buttered skillet. Bake over indirect heat, covered, for 25 to 30 minutes. Serve warm with ice cream.

Managing heat with heavy pans

Cast iron's strength, its heat retention, is also its challenge. It takes a long time to heat and a long time to cool. Pay attention:

  • Preheat gradually. Very rapid heating of cold cast iron can, rarely, cause it to crack.
  • Move, don't wait. If the pan is too hot, slide it to the cooler zone rather than waiting for it to cool on its own.
  • Use the pan's mass. A hot skillet will keep cooking food off the heat. Plan accordingly.

Safety

  • Always wear heat-resistant gloves. Cast iron handles get dangerously hot.
  • Keep a dedicated landing spot next to the grill, like a heat-safe trivet or a stone, where you can set down hot pans.
  • Be careful with fat. Hot oil and butter can flare if they spill on coals. Never fill a skillet more than a third full with oil.
  • Do not deep-fry over charcoal unless you have experience and a very stable setup.
  • Keep kids and pets well away from the grill.

Caring for cast iron after grilling

Cast iron needs a little attention after outdoor cooking:

  1. While warm, scrape out food residue with a spatula or scraper.
  2. Rinse with hot water and scrub with a stiff brush. A small amount of mild soap is fine on well-seasoned cast iron.
  3. Dry thoroughly. Set it back over the coals for a few minutes to drive off every bit of moisture.
  4. Wipe on a very thin layer of oil while warm.
  5. Store in a dry place.

Seasoning products made for carbon steel also work on cast iron. Slot Grill Seasoning Balm, for example, combines avocado oil with beeswax and is meant for both carbon steel and cast iron.

Smoke and flavor

Cooking in cast iron on a charcoal grill gives food a subtle smoky note, especially with the pan uncovered and wood chunks on the coals. Food in a covered Dutch oven absorbs less smoke. For more flavor, leave the lid off for part of the cook.

Common problems

Food sticks. The pan was not hot enough or not well seasoned. Preheat longer and use a little more fat.

Uneven heating. The pan sat over an uneven bed of coals. Rotate it every few minutes.

Rust spots after cooking. The pan was not dried thoroughly. Scrub off rust, dry and re-season.

Burned bottom on baked goods. Direct heat was too intense. Bake over indirect heat and cover the pan.

The takeaway

Cast iron turns a charcoal grill into a full outdoor kitchen. With a sturdy grill, a couple of good pans and a two-zone fire, you can sear, fry, simmer and bake outside. Start with a butter-basted steak and you will quickly find yourself cooking everything this way.

Going further: using cast iron over coals safely

Preheat gradually

Set the pan over medium coals and let it heat for several minutes. Sudden extreme heat changes can, in rare cases, crack cast iron.

Handles get hot

Every part of a cast-iron pan gets hot on a grill. Use thick gloves or a handle sleeve, and have a heat-safe spot ready to set the pan down.

Support matters

Cast iron is heavy. Make sure the grill grate can hold the weight. Heavy-gauge steel grills, like the Slot Grill, can carry the weight of a loaded cast-iron pan.

Clean up

Scrape the pan while warm, rinse with hot water, dry over the fire and wipe with oil.

Quick questions

Can I put cast iron directly on a charcoal grill?

Yes, on a sturdy, level grill. Cast iron is heavy, so the frame and grate need to support it without flexing.

How do I clean cast iron after grilling?

Scrape, rinse with hot water and scrub, dry it over the coals, then wipe on a thin coat of oil.

Can I bake on a charcoal grill in cast iron?

Yes. Use indirect heat and cover the pan to trap heat so the top cooks before the bottom burns.
