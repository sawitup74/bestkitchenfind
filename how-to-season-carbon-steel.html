Carbon Steel Care

How to Season Carbon Steel (and Why It Matters)

A step-by-step guide to building a durable, slick, rust-resistant finish on raw carbon steel grills, griddles and pans.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

Carbon steel is one of the best cooking materials there is. It is strong, conducts heat well, gets extremely hot and lasts for generations. But raw carbon steel has one weakness: it rusts. Seasoning solves that, and it also creates a naturally non-stick surface that improves with every cook. If you own a carbon steel grill, griddle or skillet, learning to season it properly is the most important maintenance skill you can have.

What seasoning actually is

Seasoning is not a coating you buy; it is a layer you build. When you heat a very thin film of fat or oil on steel, the fat goes through a chemical change called polymerization. Its molecules link together into a hard, slick, plastic-like layer that bonds to the metal. Each new layer adds to the last.

Good seasoning:

  • Protects steel from moisture and rust
  • Makes food release more easily
  • Turns steel from silver-gray to bronze, brown and eventually black
  • Gets better the more you cook

Why thin layers matter

The most common seasoning mistake is using too much oil. A thick layer does not polymerize evenly. It turns sticky, gummy and soft, and it may flake later. The goal is a layer so thin that the steel looks almost dry, with just a faint sheen.

Think of it as polishing oil into the steel rather than painting it on.

Choosing a seasoning fat

Many fats can season steel. Good options include:

  • Purpose-made seasoning products, often blends of oils and waxes.
  • Avocado oil: high smoke point, works well.
  • Canola or vegetable oil: inexpensive and effective.
  • Flaxseed oil: builds hard layers but can be brittle and flaky.
  • Shortening or lard: traditional and effective.

Seasoning a new grill or griddle

New raw steel often has a light protective oil or residue from manufacturing. Start by cleaning it.

Step 1: Clean

Wash the steel with warm, soapy water and a scrub brush or non-scratch pad. Rinse well.

Step 2: Dry completely

Dry immediately with a towel. Then heat the steel briefly in an oven or over a low fire to drive off all moisture. Raw steel can flash rust in minutes if left wet.

Step 3: Apply a very thin coat

Once the steel has cooled to warm-to-the-touch, apply a tiny amount of your seasoning product or oil using a folded paper towel or an old cotton rag. Rub it over every surface, including the edges and underside.

Then take a clean paper towel and wipe it all off as though you made a mistake. What remains is the right amount.

Step 4: Heat

Heat the steel above the smoke point of your oil. The most controlled method is a home oven at 400 to 450°F for one hour. Place the pieces upside down or on edge so excess oil can drain. Lay foil on a lower rack to catch anything that drips.

Flat-pack grills that come apart into individual plates are easy to season this way, since individual plates are far easier to fit in a home oven than an assembled grill. Measure your oven first; the longest plates on a full-size grill can be a tight fit.

If you cannot fit the pieces in an oven, you can season over a charcoal fire, heating the steel until the oil smokes and then lightly darkens. This method is less even but works well over time.

Step 5: Cool and repeat

Let the steel cool. Repeat the thin coat and heating process 2 to 4 times for a good starting base. Each layer will darken the steel slightly.

What to expect

After the first round, the steel may look golden or patchy. That is normal. Seasoning builds unevenly at first and becomes more uniform as you cook. Color variations, from straw to brown to blue-black, are not a sign of failure.

Maintenance seasoning

Every time you finish cooking:

  1. Scrape off food debris while the steel is warm.
  2. Wipe clean with a paper towel.
  3. Apply a very thin coat of oil or seasoning product.
  4. Let it heat for a few minutes on the dying fire, or store it once cool.

This small habit maintains the seasoning and prevents rust between cooks.

Seasoning the grill body

The firebox and outer surfaces of a charcoal grill do not touch food, but they are still raw steel and can rust. Some people use high-temperature barbecue paint on the outside of grills. Paint must never be used on cooking surfaces or grates. A seasoning balm offers a food-safe alternative that can be used on the entire grill, including the body, so the whole grill is treated consistently.

Foods that build seasoning faster

Cooking fatty foods helps build seasoning naturally. In the first weeks, cook:

  • Bacon
  • Burgers and sausages
  • Fried potatoes
  • Onions sautéed in oil

Foods to avoid at first

Acidic and wet foods can weaken young seasoning. Until the steel is well-seasoned, limit:

  • Tomato sauces
  • Wine or vinegar braises
  • Citrus-heavy marinades
  • Long simmers of liquids

Once seasoning is mature, these become much less of a concern, but a quick re-oil afterward is wise.

Troubleshooting seasoning

Sticky surface. Too much oil, or not enough heat. Heat again at 450°F for an hour to finish polymerizing. If it is very gummy, scrub it off and start with thinner layers.

Flaking. Layers were too thick, or the base was not clean. Scrub off the loose seasoning and re-season the area.

Patchy color. Normal. Keep cooking.

Rust spots. Scrub with steel wool or a stiff brush until clean, dry thoroughly, and season the area again.

Black residue on food. Usually carbonized food, not seasoning. Scrape and clean the surface better between cooks.

Carbon steel versus cast iron seasoning

Carbon steel and cast iron season the same way. Carbon steel is smoother and denser, so its seasoning often appears glossier and builds a little more quickly. Cast iron has a rougher surface that holds seasoning in its pores.

The takeaway

Seasoning is a simple, repeatable process: clean, dry, wipe on the thinnest possible coat, heat, and repeat. Then keep it up with a quick wipe after every cook. Do that, and your carbon steel will reward you with a slick, durable, rust-resistant surface that lasts for decades.

Going further: troubleshooting a seasoning layer

Sticky surface

A tacky, gummy finish means the oil layer was too thick or did not get hot enough for long enough. Heat the steel again at 400°F for another hour. If it is still sticky, scrub it lightly and start again with a much thinner coat.

Blotchy color

Early seasoning is almost always uneven, ranging from bronze to brown to patchy black. That is normal. It evens out with use, especially after cooking fatty foods.

Flaking

Flakes often come from thick layers that did not bond well. Scrape off the loose bits, then build several very thin layers.

Food sticks

New seasoning is still thin. Cook with a little more fat at first, let the steel preheat fully and give food time to release before flipping.

Seasoning on the grill vs. the oven

If parts of your grill do not fit in your oven, season them over a hot charcoal fire. Apply a thin coat, set the grill up, build a strong fire and let it burn for an hour. Repeat a few times.

How many layers

Three to five thin layers make a solid start. After that, everyday cooking does the rest.

Cook's notebook

A seasoning schedule

New steel: three to five oven or fire layers before first use. Afterward: a thin oil wipe after each cook and a full re-season once or twice a season.

Quick questions

How many coats of seasoning do I need?

Two to four thin coats make a good starting base. Cooking builds more over time.

My seasoning feels tacky. What went wrong?

Too much oil or not enough heat. Bake it again at about 450°F for an hour, and use thinner coats next time.

Can I use barbecue paint on my grill?

Never on cooking surfaces or grates. A food-safe seasoning balm can treat the whole grill instead.
