Marinades That Actually Work: Science, Timing and Six Recipes

What a marinade can and cannot do, how long to soak each protein, and six reliable marinades for the grill and the griddle.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

Marinades have a reputation for magic. Soak a tough steak overnight, the thinking goes, and it will emerge tender and flavorful. The truth is more modest but still useful. A well-built marinade seasons the surface, adds flavor, and can help keep lean meat juicy. Understanding what marinades really do helps you get great results without wasted time or mushy textures.

What a marinade does

Flavor mostly stays on the surface

Most marinade ingredients penetrate only a few millimeters into meat, even after many hours. Oil-soluble aromatics like garlic and herbs stay close to the surface. That is still worthwhile, because the surface is where the crust forms and where we taste first.

Salt goes deeper

Salt is the exception. Over time, salt moves into meat, seasoning it more deeply and changing its proteins so they hold more moisture during cooking. Soy sauce, fish sauce and other salty ingredients work the same way. That is why salty marinades produce juicier, more seasoned results.

Acid works on the surface, and too much can harm texture

Acids like citrus juice and vinegar change the proteins on the surface of meat. A short soak brightens flavor, but a long soak in strong acid can make the outside mushy or chalky, especially on fish and chicken.

Enzymes are powerful

Some fruits, like pineapple, papaya and kiwi, contain enzymes that break down protein. They can tenderize but quickly turn meat mushy. Use them for short marinades only.

Oil carries flavor

Oil helps distribute fat-soluble flavors from garlic, herbs and spices and helps prevent sticking on the grill.

Sugar helps browning

Honey, sugar and other sweeteners add caramelization. Watch for burning over high heat.

The basic formula

A balanced marinade includes:

  • Salt or salty ingredient: soy sauce, fish sauce, kosher salt, miso
  • Acid (in moderation): citrus juice, vinegar, yogurt, wine
  • Fat: olive oil, neutral oil, sesame oil
  • Aromatics: garlic, ginger, shallots, herbs, spices
  • Sweetness (optional): honey, brown sugar, maple syrup

A good starting ratio is about 3 parts oil to 1 part acid, with salt and aromatics to taste.

Six reliable marinades

1. Garlic-herb all-purpose marinade

Works on chicken, pork, steak and vegetables.

  • 1/2 cup olive oil
  • 3 tablespoons red wine vinegar
  • 4 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 tablespoons chopped fresh herbs (rosemary, thyme, oregano or parsley)
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon Dijon mustard

2. Soy-ginger marinade

Savory and slightly sweet. Great for steak, chicken, pork and tofu.

  • 1/2 cup soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons rice vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons honey or brown sugar
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • 1 tablespoon toasted sesame oil
  • 3 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 tablespoon grated fresh ginger
  • 2 scallions, sliced

3. Citrus-chili marinade

Bright and spicy. Perfect for chicken, pork and shrimp tacos.

  • 1/4 cup orange juice
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 1/4 cup olive oil
  • 3 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 chipotle in adobo, minced, plus 1 tablespoon adobo sauce
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons kosher salt

For shrimp, marinate only 15 to 30 minutes.

4. Yogurt-spice marinade

Yogurt is mildly acidic and coats meat well, producing tender, flavorful results with a beautiful char. Great for chicken and lamb.

  • 1 cup plain whole-milk yogurt
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 4 cloves garlic, grated
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 2 teaspoons garam masala or ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon ground coriander
  • 1 teaspoon smoked paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon turmeric
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt

5. Balsamic-rosemary marinade

Rich and tangy. Ideal for steak, portobello mushrooms and vegetables.

  • 1/4 cup balsamic vinegar
  • 1/2 cup olive oil
  • 2 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 tablespoon chopped fresh rosemary
  • 1 teaspoon Dijon mustard
  • 1 teaspoon honey
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

6. Mojo-style marinade

A garlicky citrus marinade great for pork.

  • 1/2 cup orange juice
  • 1/4 cup lime juice
  • 1/4 cup olive oil
  • 8 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 1/2 cup chopped cilantro

How to marinate

  1. Use a zip-top bag or a nonreactive container. Glass, ceramic or food-safe plastic. Avoid aluminum, which can react with acid.
  2. Coat the meat evenly. Press out the air from bags.
  3. Refrigerate. Always marinate in the refrigerator, never on the counter.
  4. Turn occasionally for even coverage.
  5. Remove and pat dry before grilling. A wet surface steams rather than sears. Patting dry produces a better crust.

Marinade safety

  • Never reuse raw marinade as a sauce without boiling it first. It contains raw meat juices.
  • Set aside a portion of fresh marinade before adding the meat if you want a sauce.
  • Discard used marinade.
  • Cook meat to safe internal temperatures; check with an instant-read thermometer.

Grilling marinated food

  • Pat dry. Better browning and less flare-up.
  • Watch for sugar. Sweet marinades burn over high heat. Use a two-zone fire: sear over coals, then move to the cooler side.
  • Oil the grates lightly. Marinated food is often wet and can stick.
  • On a griddle, marinated thin meats like skirt steak, chicken strips and shrimp cook quickly and brown beautifully.

Marinade vs. dry brine vs. rub

MethodBest forMain effect
MarinadeLean meats, bold flavors, Asian and Latin dishesSurface flavor, some moisture with salt
Dry brineSteaks, roasts, poultryDeep seasoning, juiciness, crisp skin
RubBarbecue, steaks, porkCrust and surface flavor

They can be combined. A dry brine followed by a rub is common for barbecue.

The takeaway

Marinades do not magically tenderize tough meat, but they add flavor, season the surface and help lean meats stay juicy. Build them with salt, moderate acid, oil and aromatics. Marinate in the refrigerator for the right amount of time, pat dry before cooking, and watch sugary marinades over the fire.

Going further: marinades in real life

The weeknight shortcut

Marinate meat in the morning before work, refrigerate in a sealed bag, and it is ready to grill at dinner. The soy-ginger and garlic-herb marinades are especially forgiving for an 8-hour soak.

Freeze-ahead marinade packs

Put raw chicken or pork in freezer bags with marinade, press out the air, label and freeze. The meat marinates as it thaws in the refrigerator overnight. Thaw for at least 24 hours for thick cuts.

Scoring for more flavor

Shallow cuts across the surface of thick meat, like chicken thighs or flank steak, increase surface area for the marinade and produce more crispy edges on the grill.

Marinade for vegetables

Vegetables absorb marinades quickly. Toss zucchini, mushrooms, peppers or eggplant with the balsamic-rosemary marinade for 15 to 30 minutes before grilling.

Marinades on carbon steel

Sugary and acidic marinades can stick and darken on steel. Pat food dry, oil the grates or griddle lightly, and scrape the surface clean while it is still warm after cooking.

A note on yogurt marinades

Yogurt marinades cling, which means the coating chars beautifully. Wipe off thick excess before grilling so it chars rather than steams.

Quick questions

Do marinades tenderize meat?

Only slightly, and mostly on the surface. Salt is the main ingredient that helps juiciness.

Can you marinate too long?

Yes. Strong acid or enzyme marinades can turn the surface mushy, especially on fish and chicken.

Can I use the marinade as a sauce?

Only if you boil it first, or set aside fresh marinade before adding raw meat.
