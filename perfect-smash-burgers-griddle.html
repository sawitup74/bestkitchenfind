Diner-Style Smash Burgers on a Steel Griddle

Lacy, crusty edges, melty cheese and squishy buns: the technique, the tools and the secrets behind the best burger you can make at home.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

A smash burger is a lesson in physics. Press a ball of cold, fatty beef onto a ripping-hot steel surface and the meat spreads into a thin patty with maximum contact. That contact triggers intense browning, forming a crunchy, deeply savory crust with lacy edges that no thick grilled patty can match. Two of those thin patties, stacked with melted cheese on a soft bun, make a burger that rivals the best diner counter in town.

The good news: you do not need a restaurant flat-top. A thick steel griddle over a bed of hot charcoal gets every bit as hot, and the steel holds that heat even when you drop several cold balls of beef on it.

The beef

Use 80/20 ground chuck, meaning 80 percent lean and 20 percent fat. The fat is essential: it renders and fries the patty in its own juices, creating the crust. Leaner beef sticks and turns dry.

Freshly ground beef is best. If you can, ask a butcher to coarsely grind chuck for you, or grind it yourself. Pre-packaged ground beef works fine if it is cold and fresh.

Plan on two 2- to 3-ounce patties per burger. Portion the beef into loose balls and keep them in the refrigerator until the second you cook them. Do not pack or knead them; the looser the ball, the more tender the patty.

The tools

  • A heavy steel griddle: thick carbon steel holds heat and recovers fast, which is essential for a hard sear. The Slot Grill Griddle add-on is the same heavy carbon steel as the grill, and it can replace half the grate or both halves for a full flat top.
  • A stiff, flat metal spatula with a sharp front edge for scraping the crust free. A wide, sturdy burger spatula is ideal.
  • Something to press with: a second stiff spatula stacked on top, a heavy burger press, or a small cast-iron pan.
  • Parchment paper squares to keep the beef from sticking to your press.

Heat the griddle

Light a full chimney of charcoal and spread the coals in an even layer directly under the griddle. Let the steel heat for 10 to 15 minutes. You want it extremely hot, around 450 to 500°F. Sprinkle a little water on the plate: it ought to bead up, skate around and be gone in a blink.

If your griddle is newly seasoned, wipe on a very thin layer of high-smoke-point oil. A well-seasoned griddle needs almost nothing, since the beef fat will do the work.

Prep your toppings first

Smash burgers cook in about two minutes, so have everything ready before the beef hits the steel:

  • Soft buns, such as potato rolls or brioche, split
  • American cheese slices (they melt the best; cheddar works too)
  • Thinly sliced white onion or finely diced onion
  • Dill pickle chips
  • Shredded iceberg lettuce
  • Burger sauce (see below)

Classic burger sauce

Whisk together 1/2 cup mayonnaise, 2 tablespoons ketchup, 1 tablespoon yellow mustard, 2 tablespoons finely chopped dill pickles or relish, 1 teaspoon pickle brine, 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder, 1/2 teaspoon paprika and a pinch of black pepper.

Toast the buns

Butter the cut sides of the buns and toast them face-down on the griddle for 30 to 60 seconds until golden. Set aside. Do this first so the buns are ready when the burgers are done.

Smash

  1. Place a few beef balls on the griddle, at least 5 inches apart.
  2. Lay a square of parchment on top of one ball. Immediately press down hard with your spatula and press or a second spatula stacked on top, smashing the beef into a thin patty about 1/4 inch thick. Press firmly for 10 seconds. You only get one chance: smash within the first 30 seconds, while the beef is still cold.
  3. Peel off the parchment. Season the top generously with salt and pepper.
  4. If you want the famous Oklahoma-style onion burger, pile a small handful of paper-thin sliced onions on top of the raw patty now, and smash again lightly so they press into the meat.
  5. Cook without moving for about 90 seconds to 2 minutes, until the edges are deeply browned and lacy, and you can see the color change creeping up the sides.

Scrape and flip

Here is the crucial step. The crust is stuck to the steel, and you want to keep it attached to the burger. Hold your spatula at a low angle and scrape firmly under the patty with the sharp edge, scraping up every bit of crust. Flip.

Immediately lay a slice of cheese on one patty and stack a second patty on top, with another slice of cheese. Cook for 30 seconds more, until the cheese melts. The second side does not need to brown much.

Build the burger

From the bottom up: toasted bottom bun, burger sauce, pickles, the double patty stack with melted cheese, onions, lettuce and the top bun. Eat immediately.

Seasoning variations

  • Garlic butter: brush the buns with garlic butter instead of plain.
  • Jalapeño popper: add a smear of cream cheese and sliced pickled jalapeños.
  • Patty melt: swap buns for rye bread toasted with butter, add Swiss cheese and griddled onions.
  • Mushroom Swiss: griddle sliced mushrooms with butter and thyme, pile them on the patty with Swiss cheese.
  • Bacon: griddle bacon first, then cook the burgers in the rendered fat.

Cooking for a crowd

With a full griddle on a large grill, you can cook 8 to 10 patties at once. Work in batches and keep finished burgers on a sheet pan wrapped loosely in foil for no more than a few minutes; smash burgers are best eaten immediately. Assign someone to build burgers while you cook.

Between batches, scrape the griddle clean of crust bits and pour off excess grease. Use a scraper and a folded paper towel held with tongs. Burned bits left on the steel will stick to the next batch.

Safety and doneness

Because smash burgers are very thin and cooked on very high heat, they cook through in a couple of minutes. USDA recommends cooking ground beef to 160°F. A thin smash patty easily reaches this temperature on a hot griddle; if you want to be sure, use an instant-read thermometer inserted from the side.

Troubleshooting

The patty stuck and tore when flipping. You did not scrape aggressively enough, or your spatula is too thick. A sharp, stiff spatula is essential. Make sure the griddle was hot and well-seasoned.

No crust formed. The griddle was not hot enough, or the beef was too lean. Give the griddle more time over hot coals.

Patties are thick and dense. Smash harder and faster, and do not pack the balls tightly.

Smoke is overwhelming. Beef fat smokes at high temperature. That is normal and part of the flavor, which is one more reason to cook these outside rather than in a kitchen.

Cleaning the griddle afterward

While the griddle is still hot, scrape off grease and debris with your spatula. Squirt a little water on stubborn spots and scrape; the steam lifts them. Wipe with a paper towel, then wipe on a very thin coat of oil to protect the steel. The takeaway

Cold beef, hot steel, hard press, aggressive scrape. Master those four moves and you will never order a burger out again. Smash burgers are the perfect introduction to griddle cooking, and once you taste that crust, you will understand why so many backyard cooks swear by a steel flat-top.

Cook's notebook

Getting the smash right

Press each ball flat a single time, right away, while the meat is still cold and the griddle is very hot. Use a stiff spatula and press down hard with a second spatula or a heavy press on top for leverage. A square of parchment between the meat and the press prevents sticking. After that first press, do not press again; later pressing only squeezes out juice. Flip when the edges are deeply browned and lacy, add cheese and cook another 30 to 60 seconds.

Quick questions

What beef is best for smash burgers?

80/20 ground chuck. The fat renders and fries the patty into a crisp crust.

Why use parchment when smashing?

It keeps the beef from sticking to the spatula or press so the patty spreads evenly.

How hot should the griddle be?

Very hot, roughly 450 to 500°F. Water should skitter and evaporate almost instantly.
