Carbon Steel Care

Carbon Steel vs. Cast Iron vs. Stainless Steel

How the three great cooking metals compare in heat, weight, durability and care, and why carbon steel is a favorite for grills and griddles.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

If you have spent any time reading about cookware, you have probably seen strong opinions about metals. Cast iron is beloved for its heat retention. Stainless steel is the workhorse of restaurant kitchens. Carbon steel is the chef's secret for woks, crepe pans and griddles. Each has strengths and weaknesses, and understanding them helps you choose the right tool, from a skillet to a grill.

Carbon steel

Carbon steel is iron with a small amount of carbon, typically around 1 percent or less. It is rolled into sheets and formed into pans, woks, griddles and grills.

Strengths

  • Strong and durable. Carbon steel is tough and can be formed into thin but strong shapes or thick, rigid plates.
  • Not brittle. It can take knocks and drops without cracking.
  • Takes high heat. Excellent for searing.
  • Seasons beautifully. Its smooth surface develops a slick, glossy patina.
  • Lighter than cast iron for a similar cooking surface.

Weaknesses

  • Rusts without seasoning.
  • Reactive with acids until well seasoned.
  • Needs care: cleaning, drying and oiling.

Best uses

Woks, crepe pans, paella pans, griddles, skillets and grills. Its strength makes it ideal for grill frames and grates, where it must support heavy loads at high heat.

Cast iron

Cast iron is iron with a higher carbon content, around 2 to 4 percent, poured into molds. It is thick, heavy and slightly porous.

Strengths

  • Superb heat retention. It holds a lot of heat and stays hot when food is added.
  • Even heat once preheated.
  • Seasons well.
  • Inexpensive and lasts forever.

Weaknesses

  • Very heavy.
  • Slow to heat up and cool down.
  • Brittle. It can crack if dropped or subjected to extreme temperature shock.
  • Rusts without seasoning.

Best uses

Skillets, Dutch ovens, griddles, cornbread pans and anything that benefits from heavy heat retention.

Stainless steel

Stainless steel is steel alloyed with chromium, and often nickel, that forms a protective oxide layer, preventing rust. Because stainless conducts heat relatively poorly, quality stainless cookware is usually "clad" with a core of aluminum or copper.

Strengths

  • Rust-resistant and low maintenance.
  • Non-reactive with acidic foods.
  • Dishwasher safe in most cases.
  • Good for browning and fond, the browned bits that make pan sauces.

Weaknesses

  • Food sticks without proper technique.
  • Lower heat retention than cast iron.
  • More expensive for good quality.

Best uses

Sauté pans, stockpots, saucepans and anything acidic or wet.

Why carbon steel is great for grills

Grills face punishing conditions: direct contact with burning coals, repeated heating and cooling, and the weight of food and pans. Carbon steel's combination of strength, heat tolerance and toughness makes it ideal.

Thickness matters. Thin stamped steel can warp, flex and burn through over time. Thick steel plates stay rigid. The Slot Grill uses 12-gauge carbon steel in the Compact and 10-gauge carbon steel in the Full Size, a little over an eighth of an inch thick. That heft is why the frames can carry cast-iron pans and stand up to years of use.

Seasoning works on grates too. A seasoned carbon steel grate becomes naturally slick, which makes food release more easily, much like a seasoned skillet.

Strength without brittleness. Unlike cast iron grates, carbon steel will not crack if dropped or banged around in a trunk or campsite.

Carbon steel griddles

Carbon steel griddles combine many of the best qualities of cast iron and stainless: they get very hot, hold heat well when thick, season to a non-stick surface and are tough. That is why restaurant flat-tops are often made of thick steel. A thick carbon steel griddle on a charcoal grill gives you that same restaurant-style cooking at home.

Caring for each metal

Carbon steel and cast iron

  • Clean while warm.
  • Avoid soaking.
  • Dry immediately.
  • Apply a thin coat of oil after each use.
  • Re-season as needed.
  • Do not put in the dishwasher.

Stainless steel

  • Wash with soap and water.
  • Use a stainless cleaner or a paste of baking soda for stubborn stains.
  • Dishwasher safe for most pieces.
  • No seasoning required.

Which should you buy?

If you are building a kitchen from scratch, you want at least one of each:

  • A stainless skillet and saucepan for sauces, acidic dishes and everyday cooking.
  • A cast-iron skillet or Dutch oven for searing, baking and braising.
  • A carbon steel pan or griddle for high-heat cooking and eggs.

For grilling, carbon steel is a natural choice for its strength and durability, especially if you want a grill you can travel with or store flat.

Myths

"You can never use soap on cast iron or carbon steel." Mild modern dish soap is fine on well-seasoned pans. Avoid soaking.

"Cast iron heats evenly." Cast iron is slow to heat and can have hot spots. Preheat it thoroughly.

"Stainless steel is non-stick if heated properly." Proper preheating helps food release, but stainless will never be truly non-stick.

"Carbon steel is too much work." A 30-second wipe and oil after cooking is all it takes.

The takeaway

Each metal has a place. Stainless is low maintenance and great for sauces. Cast iron excels at heat retention and baking. Carbon steel combines strength, heat tolerance and a seasoned non-stick surface, which makes it ideal for griddles and grills. Learn to season and care for it, and it will outlast nearly everything else in your kitchen.

Going further: what each metal is like to live with

Weight in practice

Weight is not just a number on a spec sheet; it changes how and where you cook. A heavy cast-iron grate or griddle is hard to lift on and off, hard to wash and awkward to pack in a car. Carbon steel is usually thinner and lighter for the same strength. Stainless can be light but tends to be thin in budget grills. For a portable grill, carbon steel offers a sweet spot of toughness and manageable weight. For reference, the Slot Grill Compact in 12-gauge carbon steel is listed at about 14 pounds, and the Full Size in 10-gauge at about 27.6 pounds.

Heat behavior

  • Cast iron heats slowly and stores huge amounts of heat. Great for searing once it is hot.
  • Carbon steel heats faster than cast iron and responds more quickly when the heat changes.
  • Stainless steel conducts heat poorly on its own and often develops hot spots unless it is layered with another metal.

Maintenance in practice

Cast iron and carbon steel both need a seasoning layer of polymerized oil and both can rust if left wet. Seasoning is a habit, not a chore: dry the steel, wipe on a very thin layer of oil and heat it. Stainless needs no seasoning, but food sticks to it more readily, and it can discolor with heat.

Durability

Cast iron is strong but brittle; a dropped grate or pan can crack. Carbon steel bends rather than cracks under abuse and can almost always be restored after rust. Stainless resists corrosion but thinner stainless can warp.

Which to choose for a grill body

For a charcoal grill body that travels, takes a beating and holds heavy cookware, thick carbon steel is hard to beat. It can be seasoned like a pan, restored when neglected and is rugged enough to set a cast-iron skillet over the coals.

Which to choose for pans

Most serious home cooks own all three: stainless for sauces and acidic dishes, cast iron for deep frying and baking, and carbon steel for fast searing, eggs and stir-fries.

Quick questions

Is carbon steel better than cast iron?

Neither is better overall. Carbon steel is lighter and tougher; cast iron holds more heat.

Does carbon steel need seasoning?

Yes. Seasoning prevents rust and creates a slick, non-stick surface.

Why do grills use carbon steel?

It is strong, tolerates very high heat and will not crack like cast iron, especially in thick gauges.
