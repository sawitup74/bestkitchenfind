The Two-Zone Fire: Grilling's Master Skill

One side hot, one side calm. How to build, read and use a two-zone charcoal fire for steaks, chicken, fish and everything in between.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

If you could learn only one grilling skill, it should be the two-zone fire. It is the difference between food that is charred outside and raw inside, and food that is perfectly cooked through with a beautiful sear. It is how professional cooks handle thick steaks, bone-in chicken, sausages, and anything that would burn over constant high heat. It is also how they rescue food from flare-ups.

The concept is simple: put all your coals on one side of the grill, and leave the other side empty. You now have a hot zone for searing and a cool zone for gentle cooking. This guide explains how to build one, how to judge the heat, and how to use it for almost anything.

Why a single layer of coals is not enough

Most beginners spread their coals evenly across the whole grill. The result is one temperature everywhere. Thin foods cook fine. But anything thicker than about an inch, or anything with fat that drips, becomes a gamble. The outside burns before the inside finishes, and when fat hits the coals, flames shoot up with nowhere to escape.

A two-zone fire gives you choices. You can move food from hot to cool and back again, controlling the cooking like you would by turning a stove knob.

How heat moves on a grill

There are three kinds of heat at work:

  • Radiant heat comes directly from glowing coals, like the warmth of sunlight. It is what browns food sitting directly over the fire.
  • Conductive heat passes from the hot grate into the food, creating grill marks.
  • Convective heat is hot air circulating around the food. On the coal-free half of a two-zone fire, convection and indirect radiant heat do most of the cooking.

On the hot side, food gets all three in large doses. On the cool side, it gets gentle radiant heat from the nearby coals and warm air, much like an oven.

Building a two-zone fire

  1. Light the charcoal in a chimney starter. Fill the chimney with as much charcoal as you need: half for a short cook of sausages or chops, full for steaks, chicken or longer cooks. Light it with newspaper or a natural fire starter.
  2. Wait for the coals to ash over. This takes 15 to 20 minutes. The top layer should be covered in a light gray ash.
  3. Pour the coals onto one side of the grill. On a rectangular grill, pile them against one short end, covering about half to two-thirds of the charcoal bed. Bank them slightly so they are deeper at the wall and taper toward the middle.
  4. Set the grate and preheat. Let the grate heat for 5 to 10 minutes. Brush it clean, then oil it with a paper towel dipped in oil and held with tongs.

Variations on the layout

Classic two-zone

Coals on one side, nothing on the other. Best for most things.

Three-zone

Pile coals thick on one end, thinner in the middle, and leave the other end empty. This gives you high, medium and low heat at the same time. Great when you cook different foods at once, such as steak, vegetables and bread.

Center channel

Put coals on both outer sides and leave a cool channel in the middle, or the reverse. Useful for long foods like a rack of ribs or a whole fish, or when you need a cool zone surrounded by heat.

Ring of fire

Arrange coals around the edges with an empty center. Good for a whole chicken or a roast you want to cook evenly from all sides.

Reading the heat with your hand

You do not need a thermometer to judge grill heat. Hold your open palm about 5 inches above the grate and count how many seconds you can keep it there comfortably.

SecondsHeat levelApprox. temp
2High450–550°F
4Medium-high400–450°F
6Medium350–400°F
8–10Low250–325°F

Do this test over both zones before you start cooking. It is not precise, but it is reliable once you calibrate your sense of it.

What to cook in each zone

Hot zone (direct heat):

  • Thin steaks, burgers, chops
  • Shrimp, scallops
  • Skewers and kebabs
  • Vegetables that cook quickly
  • Searing thick foods at the end

Cool zone (indirect heat):

  • Thick steaks (before searing)
  • Bone-in chicken
  • Whole chickens and roasts
  • Pork tenderloin finishing
  • Sausages and brats
  • Salmon on a plank
  • Foods with sugary glazes
  • Holding cooked food warm

Common techniques

Sear and slide

Sear food over direct heat to build color, then move it to the cool side to finish cooking. Best for medium-thick foods like pork chops, chicken breasts and sausages.

Reverse sear

Cook food slowly on the cool side first, then finish with a sear over the coals. Ideal for very thick steaks and roasts. The slow start dries the surface and evens out the internal temperature, so the final sear is fast and dramatic.

Flare-up escape

When fat drips and flames jump up, slide the food away from the coals and wait for the flare to settle. Never use water to put out a grease flare-up; it can splash and spread the fire.

Glazing

Brush sugary sauces on food only after it is mostly cooked, and keep it on the cool side so the sugar caramelizes without burning.

Keeping the fire going

Charcoal burns down over time. For cooks longer than about 45 minutes, feed a few new coals onto the hot side every 30 to 45 minutes. Unlit briquettes take 10 to 15 minutes to catch, so add them before the fire drops. For faster recovery, light a half chimney of fresh charcoal on a heat-safe surface and add the lit coals.

Controlling temperature without a lid

On an open grill, your main tools are:

  • The amount of charcoal. More coals mean more heat.
  • Coal arrangement. A tight pile is hotter than a spread-out layer.
  • Distance. Food at the edge of the hot zone gets less heat than food directly over the center.
  • Covering food. A foil tent or an inverted metal pan over food on the cool side traps heat and speeds cooking.

Safety

  • Put the grill on level ground that cannot catch fire, away from buildings, overhanging branches and anything that can catch fire. A raised design with an air gap underneath, like the Slot Grill's, helps keep heat off the surface below, but still use common sense.
  • Keep a bucket of sand or a fire extinguisher nearby.
  • Never use lighter fluid on lit coals.
  • Let coals burn out completely, or extinguish them with water and stir, before disposing of the ashes in a metal container.

The takeaway

Once you master the two-zone fire, grilling stops being a guessing game. You have a hot side to sear and a cool side to cook gently, and you can move food back and forth to control the outcome. Practice it on a weeknight with something simple, like sausages or chicken thighs, and you will soon use it for everything.

Cook's notebook

Two-zone fire on a small grill

On a compact grill, the cool zone is small, so arrange coals tightly against one end and leave the other third or so bare. Turn food often and rotate it so the side facing the coals changes. Use the thermometer more frequently, since smaller grills can swing temperature quickly. On a grill without a lid, such as a Slot Grill, indirect cooking is gentler and slower than in a covered kettle, so thick cuts take longer. Plan for it, give yourself extra time, and cover large pieces loosely with an inverted foil pan or a sheet of foil if you need to trap a little heat. Small fires reward attention, but they also respond quickly when you adjust, which makes them wonderful teachers.

Quick questions

What is a two-zone fire?

Charcoal piled on one side of the grill and the other side left empty, creating a hot direct zone and a cooler indirect zone.

How do I check grill heat without a thermometer?

Hold your palm about 5 inches above the grate and count seconds: 2 is high, 4 medium-high, 6 medium, 8 to 10 low.

How do I handle flare-ups?

Move food off the coals and let the flare settle. Do not spray water on grease fires.
