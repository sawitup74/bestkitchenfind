Charcoal 101: Lump, Briquettes and the Chimney Starter

What charcoal actually is, how the two main types behave, how much to use, and the no-chemical way to light it every time.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

Charcoal is the engine of every cookout, and yet most people buy whatever bag is on sale and dump lighter fluid on it. Understanding your fuel will make you a better cook immediately. Different charcoals burn at different temperatures and for different lengths of time, produce different amounts of ash, and add slightly different flavors. Here is everything a home cook needs to know.

What charcoal is

Charcoal is wood that has been heated in a low-oxygen environment until most of its water and volatile compounds are driven off. What remains is mostly carbon. Because the compounds that create heavy, sooty smoke have already been burned away, charcoal burns hotter and cleaner than raw wood, with a steady glow instead of big flames.

That clean, intense heat is why charcoal is such a good cooking fuel. It sears beautifully and adds a subtle flavor that comes from fat and juices dripping onto the hot coals and vaporizing back up into the food.

Lump charcoal

Lump charcoal is made from pieces of real wood carbonized whole. Open a bag and you will see irregular chunks that look like burnt branches and boards.

Pros:

  • Lights quickly, usually in 10 to 15 minutes
  • Burns very hot, great for searing
  • Produces little ash
  • Made from wood with few or no additives
  • Responds quickly to more airflow

Cons:

  • Burns faster, so long cooks need refueling
  • Irregular sizes make heat less predictable
  • Bags often contain dust and tiny pieces at the bottom
  • Can spark and pop when first lit

Briquettes

Briquettes are made from ground charcoal mixed with a binder, usually starch, and pressed into uniform pillow shapes. Some include additives to help them light or burn evenly. "Instant light" briquettes are soaked in lighter fluid and should be avoided for cooking because they can leave off flavors.

Pros:

  • Uniform size gives steady, predictable heat
  • Burn longer than lump
  • Easy to measure and replicate
  • Usually less expensive

Cons:

  • Take longer to light, about 15 to 25 minutes
  • Produce more ash
  • Burn at a lower peak temperature
  • Some contain fillers

Which should you use?

Both are excellent. Choose based on the cook.

CookBest fuel
Steaks, burgers, searingLump
Quick weeknight dinnersLump
Long cooks, chili, Dutch ovensBriquettes
Beginners who want consistencyBriquettes
Griddle cookingEither; briquettes for long sessions

Many experienced grillers mix the two: a base of briquettes for steady burn, with lump on top for a fast, hot start.

Why skip lighter fluid

Lighter fluid is petroleum-based. If food goes on before it has fully burned off, it can taste like chemicals. It also creates a burst of flames that is unpredictable and potentially dangerous. And it is completely unnecessary. A chimney starter lights charcoal faster and more evenly with nothing but paper.

How to use a chimney starter

A chimney starter is a steel tube with a handle on the side and a rack set low inside. It works by drawing air up through the coals, like a smokestack.

  1. Crumple two sheets of newspaper loosely and stuff them in the bottom chamber, under the grate. You can drizzle a teaspoon of vegetable oil on the paper to help it burn longer. Alternatively, use a natural paraffin-free fire starter cube or wood wool.
  2. Fill the top chamber with charcoal.
  3. Set the chimney on the charcoal grate of your grill or on a non-flammable surface like concrete.
  4. Light the paper in several places.
  5. Wait. Flames will rise through the charcoal. In 10 to 20 minutes, the top coals will be glowing and covered with gray ash.
  6. Wearing a heat-resistant glove, grab the handle and pour the coals into the grill in your desired arrangement.

Other lighting methods

Electric starter: a looped heating element you bury in charcoal. It needs an outlet but uses no paper.

Natural fire starters: wax-and-wood cubes or wood wool twists. Place one or two under a pile of charcoal or in the chimney.

Torch: a propane torch can light a small amount of lump charcoal quickly. Hold it to the bottom of the coals for a minute or two. Use carefully.

Avoid gasoline, kerosene or any other accelerant.

Signs coals are ready

  • The top coals are covered in a light gray ash.
  • Flames have died down and the coals are glowing orange inside.
  • The heat feels intense when you hold your hand over them.

Cooking over coals that are still black and flaming leads to sooty, bitter-tasting food.

Refueling

For longer cooks, add fresh charcoal before the fire dies down. Add unlit briquettes to the edge of the lit pile; they will catch in about 15 minutes. If you need heat faster, light a half chimney on a non-flammable surface and add it once ashed over.

Storing charcoal

Charcoal absorbs moisture, which makes it hard to light and causes popping and sparks. Store bags off the ground in a dry place, ideally in a sealed plastic or metal container with a lid. If charcoal gets damp, spread it out in the sun to dry.

Putting out and disposing of coals

Never dump hot coals into a trash can or onto grass. Coals can stay hot for many hours, even a full day.

  1. Let the coals burn out completely, or carefully douse them with water, stirring to make sure every piece is wet.
  2. Wait until the ashes are completely cold to the touch.
  3. Dispose of them in a metal container, never plastic.

On a Slot Grill, once the steel and ashes are fully cool, you can dump the ash and slide the plates apart to store them flat.

Charcoal and flavor

Charcoal itself contributes a clean, subtle smokiness. Most of the "grilled" flavor comes from drippings sizzling on the coals and from browning on the food's surface. For more smoke flavor, add a small chunk of hardwood like hickory, oak, apple or cherry to the coals. Two or three fist-sized chunks are plenty for a typical cook.

Safety essentials

  • Grill only outdoors, in a well-ventilated area. Charcoal produces carbon monoxide.
  • Stand the grill on solid, level ground, clear of anything that can burn.
  • Keep children and pets away from the hot grill and chimney.
  • Have a fire extinguisher or bucket of sand nearby.
  • Wear heat-resistant gloves when handling a chimney.

The takeaway

Lump for heat, briquettes for endurance, and a chimney starter for clean, easy lighting. Learn how much your grill needs for different cooks, and you will have total control over your fire. Ditch the lighter fluid once and for all; your food will taste better for it.

Going further: charcoal storage and buying

Keep it dry

Damp charcoal is hard to light and burns poorly. Store charcoal in a sealed, waterproof container off the ground.

How much to buy

A full chimney typically handles a big cook on a large portable grill. A half chimney is often enough for smaller grills or quick meals.

Reusing charcoal

Unburned charcoal can be reused. Close off airflow or let the fire die naturally, then shake off the ash and save the leftover pieces for next time.

Lighting without lighter fluid

A chimney starter makes lighter fluid unnecessary. Stuff two sheets of newspaper or a natural fire starter in the bottom, load the upper chamber with charcoal and light the paper underneath. Quick questions

Is lump charcoal better than briquettes?

Neither is better overall. Lump lights fast and burns hot; briquettes burn longer and steadier.

How long does a chimney starter take?

About 10 to 20 minutes until the top coals are ashed over.

How do I safely dispose of charcoal ash?

Let it cool completely or douse it with water, then put it in a metal container. Coals can stay hot for many hours.
