Spatchcocked Whole Chicken Over Charcoal

Flatten the bird, salt it overnight, and cook it over a split fire for crackly skin and juicy meat in under an hour.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

A whole roast chicken is the ultimate comfort food, and a whole chicken cooked over charcoal is even better. The problem is shape: a round bird has thick thighs that need more time and delicate breasts that need less, and in a hot grill the breast dries out long before the legs are done. Spatchcocking solves this. By removing the backbone and pressing the bird flat, you put all the meat at the same height, expose all the skin to the heat, and cut the cooking time nearly in half.

What spatchcocking is

Spatchcocking, also called butterflying, means cutting out the backbone of a poultry carcass and flattening it. The legs end up on the outside edges, where the heat of a two-zone fire hits them most, while the breast sits in the middle. Everything finishes at about the same time.

It takes about two minutes with a good pair of kitchen shears.

Choosing your chicken

A 3.5 to 4.5-pound bird is the sweet spot. Larger birds take longer and may not fit a smaller grill. Once flattened, a 4-pound chicken is roughly 12 inches across and 10 inches tall, which fits comfortably on the Slot Grill Full Size's 20.5 × 11.25 in. grate with room for the coals to sit off to one side. On the Compact, choose a smaller bird of around 3 to 3.5 pounds, or cook two halved Cornish hens instead.

Air-chilled chickens have less retained water and brown better. If you can find them, they are worth it.

How to spatchcock a chicken

  1. Place the chicken breast-side down on a cutting board with the neck end facing you.
  2. Using sturdy kitchen shears, cut along one side of the backbone from tail to neck.
  3. Cut along the other side of the backbone and remove it. Save it in the freezer for stock.
  4. Flip the chicken over so it is breast-side up. Press firmly on the center of the breastbone with the heel of your hand until you hear a crack and the bird lies flat.
  5. Tuck the wing tips behind the breast so they do not burn.

Dry brine overnight

Salting the chicken a day ahead is the single best thing you can do for juicy meat and crisp skin.

Use 1 teaspoon of kosher salt per pound of chicken. Mix the salt with a teaspoon of baking powder (not baking soda), which raises the pH of the skin and helps it brown and blister. Pat the bird dry and sprinkle the mixture over every surface, including under the skin of the breast and thighs where you can reach.

Set the chicken on a rack over a sheet pan and refrigerate uncovered for 12 to 24 hours. The skin will look dry and slightly translucent.

Seasoning

Right before grilling, brush with a little oil and season. Keep it simple with black pepper and garlic powder, or use a rub. A few ideas:

  • Herb butter: soften 4 tablespoons of butter with minced garlic, thyme, rosemary and lemon zest. Rub it under the skin before cooking.
  • Smoky paprika: smoked paprika, garlic powder, onion powder, black pepper and a pinch of cayenne.
  • Lemon pepper: dried lemon peel, cracked pepper and dried thyme.
  • Za'atar: a spoonful of za'atar mixed with olive oil, rubbed all over.

The fire

Build a two-zone fire with a twist: put the coals on one side, and arrange the chicken with its legs pointing toward the heat. Because dark meat needs a higher final temperature than white meat, giving the thighs and drumsticks a little more heat evens out the cooking.

Light a full chimney of charcoal. When the coals are covered in gray ash, pour them onto one side of the grill. Clean and oil the grate.

Cooking

  1. Place the chicken skin-side up on the cool side, legs facing the coals.
  2. Cook for about 30 minutes without touching it. Fat will render and the skin will begin to tighten and color.
  3. Check the temperature. You are looking for about 150°F in the thickest part of the breast and 165°F in the thigh before moving on.
  4. Flip the chicken skin-side down and move it over direct heat. Cook 3 to 6 minutes, watching closely and moving it if flames flare, until the skin is crisp and deep golden brown.
  5. Flip back skin-side up. Pull the chicken when the breast reads 160 to 165°F and the thighs read at least 175°F.

Total cooking time is typically 40 to 55 minutes. If the bird stalls on the cool side, toss a few new coals onto the pile.

On an open grill without a lid, the top of the chicken gets less heat. If your bird is cooking slowly, tent it loosely with a sheet of foil during the first phase. Remove the foil before crisping the skin.

Resting and carving

Rest the chicken skin-side up on a cutting board for 10 minutes. To carve:

  1. Cut through the skin between the thigh and the breast and pop the leg quarter off at the joint. Separate the drumstick from the thigh.
  2. Cut through the wing joint to remove each wing.
  3. Slice along the breastbone and remove each breast half in one piece. Slice crosswise.

Pour any juices on the board over the meat.

Pan sauce from the drippings

Set a cast-iron skillet under the chicken during the first phase of cooking to catch drippings. After removing the bird, put the skillet over the coals, add a minced shallot, a splash of white wine and a cup of chicken stock, and simmer until reduced by half. Swirl in a tablespoon of butter and a squeeze of lemon. Pour over the carved chicken.

Side dishes that share the grill

  • Smashed potatoes: boil small potatoes until tender, smash them flat, brush with oil and grill or griddle until crisp.
  • Charred broccolini or broccoli: toss with oil and salt and grill over direct heat until charred at the edges. Finish with lemon and chili flakes.
  • Grilled bread salad: cube grilled crusty bread, toss with tomatoes, cucumbers, red onion, basil, olive oil and red wine vinegar.
  • Corn on the cob with herb butter.

Variations

Peri-peri chicken: blend red chilies, garlic, smoked paprika, lemon juice, oregano and olive oil into a hot sauce. Rub on the bird before grilling and baste with more during cooking.

Jerk-style chicken: rub with a paste of scallions, Scotch bonnet pepper, allspice, thyme, garlic, ginger and soy sauce. Marinate overnight.

Chimichurri chicken: grill simply seasoned, then serve with a bright, garlicky chimichurri spooned over the carved meat.

Tandoori-inspired: marinate in yogurt with tandoori spice, garlic, ginger and lemon for 6 to 12 hours. The yogurt chars beautifully.

Turkey and other birds

The same method works for a small turkey, 10 to 12 pounds, as long as your grill can hold it. Turkeys need much more time and fuel. Cornish game hens are a great small-grill option: spatchcock them and cook for about 25 to 30 minutes total.

Using the leftovers

Shred leftover chicken for tacos, enchiladas, chicken salad, or noodle soup. Simmer the carcass with the reserved backbone, onions, carrots, celery, peppercorns and bay leaves for three hours to make a rich stock.

Troubleshooting

The skin is rubbery. The skin was wet or the bird never got enough direct heat. Dry brine uncovered overnight and crisp skin-side down over hot coals at the end.

The breast is dry. You overcooked it. Pull the breast at 160 to 165°F and let carryover finish it.

The thighs are underdone. Angle the legs toward the fire and give them extra direct heat at the end.

Flare-ups during crisping. Move the bird to the edge of the fire and back again as needed. Trimming excess fat helps.

The takeaway

Spatchcocking is one of the most useful skills in grilling. It is fast, foolproof once you have done it, and it produces a chicken that is juicier and crispier than anything you will make in an oven. Make it your Sunday ritual.

Cook's notebook

Carving

Cut the bird into halves along the breastbone, then separate legs and wings. Slice the breast across the grain.

Quick questions

What does spatchcock mean?

Removing the backbone and pressing the chicken flat so it cooks evenly and faster.

Why add baking powder to the salt?

It raises the skin's pH, which helps it dry, brown and blister into a crisp crust.

How long does a spatchcocked chicken take on a charcoal grill?

Usually 40 to 55 minutes for a 4-pound bird over a two-zone fire.
