Five Homemade Rubs Every Griller Should Know

An all-purpose barbecue rub, a steak rub, a poultry blend, a coffee-chili rub and a Mediterranean herb mix, plus the rules of rubbing.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

A good spice rub is like a secret weapon. It adds flavor, creates a crust and gives your food a signature taste. Store-bought rubs are convenient, but homemade rubs are fresher, cheaper, customizable and free of anti-caking agents and fillers. This guide covers the basics of building a rub, five versatile recipes and how to use them.

Anatomy of a rub

Most rubs have a few core components:

  • Salt: the most important flavor enhancer. It also seasons the meat and helps it retain moisture.
  • Sugar: adds sweetness and helps create caramelization and crust. Brown sugar, white sugar, turbinado and coconut sugar all work.
  • Heat: black pepper, cayenne, chili powder, chipotle and red pepper flakes.
  • Aromatics: garlic powder, onion powder.
  • Character: paprika, cumin, coriander, mustard, herbs, coffee.

The salt question

Many recipes include salt in the rub. That is convenient, but it ties the amount of seasoning to the amount of flavor. Some cooks prefer to salt meat separately, ideally ahead of time as a dry brine, and use a salt-free rub. Both approaches work. For simplicity, the recipes here include salt. If you dry brine, cut or omit the salt.

The sugar question

Sugar caramelizes and builds a dark crust, but it burns at high heat. For hot, fast grilling, such as steaks and burgers, use little or no sugar. For indirect, slower cooking, like chicken or pork on the cool side, sugar works well.

Rub 1: All-purpose barbecue rub

Sweet, smoky and savory. Great on pork, chicken, ribs and vegetables.

  • 1/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1/4 cup smoked paprika
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon garlic powder
  • 1 tablespoon onion powder
  • 1 teaspoon dry mustard
  • 2 teaspoons chili powder
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne

Rub 2: Bold steak rub

Coarse and savory, without sugar so it will not burn at high heat.

  • 2 tablespoons coarse kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons coarsely cracked black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon granulated garlic
  • 1 teaspoon onion powder
  • 1 teaspoon crushed red pepper flakes
  • 1 teaspoon dried rosemary, crushed
  • 1 teaspoon coriander seed, crushed

Rub 3: Lemon-herb poultry rub

Bright and herby. Great on chicken, turkey and fish.

  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon dried lemon peel or the zest of 2 lemons (if using fresh zest, use within a few days)
  • 1 tablespoon dried thyme
  • 1 tablespoon dried oregano
  • 2 teaspoons garlic powder
  • 2 teaspoons onion powder
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon sweet paprika

Rub 4: Coffee-chili rub

Dark and earthy. Perfect for beef and pork.

  • 2 tablespoons finely ground dark roast coffee
  • 2 tablespoons ancho chili powder
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon smoked paprika
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon cumin
  • 1 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon
  • 1/4 teaspoon cayenne

Rub 5: Mediterranean herb rub

Fragrant and versatile. Great on lamb, chicken, fish and vegetables.

  • 2 tablespoons dried oregano
  • 1 tablespoon dried thyme
  • 1 tablespoon dried rosemary, crushed
  • 1 tablespoon garlic powder
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 2 teaspoons ground coriander
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon sumac (optional)
  • 1/2 teaspoon red pepper flakes

How to make a rub

  1. Measure everything into a bowl.
  2. Whisk or mix with your fingers to break up any clumps, especially from brown sugar.
  3. For the best flavor, toast whole spices like cumin and coriander in a dry skillet until fragrant, then grind them.
  4. Store in an airtight jar.

How to apply a rub

  1. Pat the meat dry.
  2. Add a binder (optional). A very thin layer of oil, mustard or mayonnaise helps the rub stick. The binder flavor mostly disappears during cooking.
  3. Sprinkle the rub evenly from about a foot above the meat for even coverage.
  4. Press it in gently. Do not literally rub, which can cause clumps; pat it in.
  5. Coat all sides, including edges.

How much rub

A good rule is about 1 tablespoon of rub per pound of meat, adjusted to taste. Lean cuts can take more; thin cuts need less.

When to apply

  • Right before cooking: for quick cooks and when using a rub without salt.
  • Several hours to overnight: for rubs with salt, which act as a dry brine and season more deeply.

Storing rubs

  • Store in airtight glass jars away from heat, light and moisture.
  • Ground spices lose potency after about 6 months. Make rubs in small batches.
  • Brown sugar can clump; add a slice of bread or a clay sugar saver to keep it soft.
  • Label jars with the name and date.

Rub troubleshooting

Burnt rub. Too much sugar for high-heat cooking. Use a sugar-free rub for steaks or cook over indirect heat.

Bland rub. Old spices. Buy fresh and toast whole spices.

Too salty. You salted and used a salted rub. Choose one or the other.

Rub fell off. The surface was wet, or there was no binder. Pat dry and use a thin binder.

The takeaway

With five rubs in your pantry, you are ready for almost any grill cook. Make them in small batches, store them well, match them to the food, and apply them with care. Soon you will be developing your own signature blends.

Going further: building your own signature blend

Start from a ratio

Once you understand the core, you can invent rubs from a simple ratio. A reliable starting point for a barbecue blend:

  • 2 parts sugar
  • 2 parts paprika
  • 1 part salt
  • 1 part pepper
  • 1 part aromatics (garlic and onion)
  • 1/2 part accent spices (cumin, mustard, chili, herbs)

For steak and other high-heat cooks, drop the sugar and double the pepper.

Toast, then grind

Whole spices bloom when toasted. Put cumin, coriander, fennel or peppercorns in a dry skillet over medium heat and shake until they are fragrant and a shade darker, about 2 minutes. Cool and grind in a spice grinder or with a mortar and pestle. The difference over pre-ground spices is huge.

Grind size matters

Coarse rubs create a rugged crust on steaks and brisket-style cuts. Fine rubs dissolve into a smoother coating, better for chicken and fish. Adjust by grinding coarsely or finely and by choosing granulated or powdered garlic.

Rubs on the griddle

A griddle is a great place to use rubs on thin cuts: chicken cutlets, shrimp, pork medallions or sliced vegetables. Because the flat steel contacts the whole surface, sugar-heavy rubs can burn quickly. Keep heat at medium, use a little oil and turn food often.

Rubs beyond meat

  • Sprinkle the all-purpose rub on popcorn tossed in melted butter.
  • Toss potato wedges in oil and the coffee-chili rub before grilling.
  • Mix the Mediterranean rub with olive oil for a dip for bread.
  • Season roasted nuts with the barbecue rub.
  • Add a pinch of the poultry rub to mayonnaise for a quick sandwich spread.

Keep a rub log

When you make a rub, write the recipe and your notes in a notebook: what you cooked, how it tasted and what you would change. In a season you will have refined a house blend that is entirely yours.

Gift jars

Rubs make easy, inexpensive gifts. Use small 4-ounce jars, a printed label, a list of ingredients for allergy awareness and two or three suggested uses.

Cook's notebook

Rub safety and allergies

When you share rubs, label common allergens. Some chili powders and spice blends include additives, and some people are sensitive to mustard, sesame or celery seed. Never use a rub shaker directly over raw meat and then over cooked food; pour what you need into a small bowl and discard leftovers that touched raw meat. Measure into a separate dish before you start rubbing, and you will keep the main jar clean for next time. These small habits protect guests and keep your blend fresh for months.

Quick questions

How much rub should I use per pound of meat?

About 1 tablespoon per pound, adjusted to taste and thickness.

Should I apply a rub the night before?

If the rub contains salt, yes; it acts as a dry brine. Salt-free rubs can go on just before cooking.

Why does my rub burn?

Sugar burns at high heat. Use sugar-free rubs for hot, fast cooking.
