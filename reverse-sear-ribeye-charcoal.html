Reverse-Sear Ribeye Over Charcoal

The thick-cut steak method that gives you edge-to-edge pink and a crackling crust, with a two-zone fire doing all the hard work.

8 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

Most people cook a steak backward. They throw a cold slab over the hottest part of the fire, flip it a few times, and hope the middle catches up before the outside turns to leather. With a thin steak that can work. With a ribeye that is an inch and a half thick or more, it almost never does. You end up with a gray ring a quarter inch deep, a cool center, and a crust that tastes slightly burnt.

The reverse sear flips the script. You cook the steak gently first, on the coal-free half of a two-zone charcoal fire, until the inside is a few degrees shy of where you want it. Only then do you move it over blazing coals for a short, violent sear. The result is a steak that is evenly rosy from top to bottom with a dark, savory crust. It is the single most reliable way we know to cook a thick steak at home, and a charcoal grill is the ideal place to do it.

Why the reverse sear works

Two things happen when you cook a steak. Heat travels inward and slowly raises the temperature of the muscle, and the surface browns through the Maillard reaction. Those two processes want different conditions. Gentle, indirect heat is best for the interior because it narrows the temperature gap between the edge and the center. Browning wants a dry surface and very high heat.

Doing the gentle part first also dries the surface of the meat. By the time you move the steak to the hot side, the exterior has lost much of its moisture, so the energy from the coals goes straight into browning instead of boiling off water. That is why a reverse-seared steak develops a crust in about a minute per side instead of four or five.

There is a bonus: the slow start gives you a wide margin for error. A steak climbing a degree or two per minute is easy to catch at the right moment. A steak screaming over direct heat can blow past medium-rare while you look for the tongs.

Picking the steak

Buy thick. The method shines with cuts at least 1.5 inches thick and really sings at 2 inches. Thin steaks do not need it, and they will overcook during the sear no matter what you do.

  • Ribeye is the classic choice. Its fat cap and marbling render beautifully during the slow phase.
  • Bone-in ribeye (sometimes sold as a cowboy cut) works too; the bone slows cooking near it, so check temperature away from the bone.
  • New York strip is leaner and firmer, with a strong beefy flavor.
  • Thick top sirloin is the budget move. Treat it exactly the same and slice it thin against the grain.

Look for bright red meat, firm white fat, and fine streaks of marbling throughout the eye rather than a few big seams.

Dry brine the night before

Salt is the most important seasoning, and timing matters. Salt a steak right before cooking and it is fine. Salt it 40 minutes ahead and the surface is wet with drawn-out juices, which hurts browning. Salt it the night before and the brine has been reabsorbed, seasoning the meat deeper and leaving a dry surface.

Use roughly three-quarters of a teaspoon of kosher salt per pound. Sprinkle it evenly on every side, including the edges, and set the steak on a rack over a plate, uncovered, in the refrigerator for 12 to 24 hours. The surface will look slightly darker and tacky. That is exactly what you want.

Pepper can go on now or just before cooking. Garlic powder or a light dusting of your favorite rub can also go on before grilling, but keep anything sugary off, since sugar will scorch during the sear.

Building the two-zone fire

You need one side of the grill packed with hot coals and the other side empty. On the Slot Grill Full Size, the 20.5 × 11.25 in. grate gives you plenty of room: bank lit charcoal against one short end and leave the other half bare. On the Compact, which has a 16.25 by 9 inch grate, push the coals to one end and keep the steak toward the opposite end. One large ribeye or two smaller strips fit comfortably on the cool side.

  1. Fill a chimney starter about two-thirds full of charcoal and light it with crumpled newspaper or a natural fire starter.
  2. Give it time, until a gray film of ash covers the top coals, usually 15 to 20 minutes.
  3. Pour the coals into one half of the grill and pile them so they sit closer to the grate.
  4. Set the grate in place and let it heat for five minutes, then scrape it clean.

For the slow phase, the air temperature over the cool side should land somewhere around 250 to 300°F. You do not need to measure it precisely. If you can hold your hand a few inches above the cool side for six to eight seconds, you are in the zone.

The slow phase

Pull the steak from the refrigerator, pat it dry, and add pepper. Lay it on the cool side with the fat cap facing the coals. Leave it alone.

Check the internal temperature every 8 to 10 minutes with an instant-read thermometer, inserting the probe horizontally from the side so the tip lands in the very center. A 1.5-inch steak will take roughly 25 to 40 minutes; a 2-inch steak can take 45 minutes or longer. The variables are your fire, the starting temperature of the meat, and the weather, so cook to temperature, not to the clock.

Flip the steak once halfway through and rotate it so the other edge faces the fire. That evens out the cooking.

Wake up the fire, then sear

While the steak finishes its slow phase, give the coals a boost. Drop a few new coals onto the lit pile about ten minutes before you expect to sear, or simply consolidate the existing coals into a tighter mound right under one section of the grate. You want the hottest possible spot.

When the steak hits its pull temperature, move it directly over the coals. Sear 45 to 75 seconds per side, flipping several times if you like. Use tongs to hold it on its edge and render the fat cap for another 30 seconds. Watch for flare-ups from dripping fat; if flames lick up, slide the steak over a few inches and let them die down.

A cast-iron skillet set right on the grate over the coals is another great searing option. It gives you even, all-over contact and lets you baste with butter, garlic and thyme for the last minute. The Slot Grill frame is strong enough to carry a heavy skillet without flexing.

Resting and slicing

Because the interior was cooked gently, a reverse-seared steak needs only a short rest. Five minutes on a board is plenty. The juices will barely pool.

Slice against the grain into half-inch strips. For a ribeye, separate the cap (the curved outer muscle, the best bite on the cow) from the eye and slice each separately. Finish with a pinch of flaky salt.

Easy sides that share the fire

Thick steaks deserve a couple of sides that do not demand your attention while you babysit the thermometer.

  • Coal-roasted potatoes: wrap small potatoes in foil with butter, salt and rosemary and nestle the packet beside the coals during the slow phase. Turn once.
  • Grilled asparagus or green beans: toss with oil and salt and char over direct heat right after the steak comes off.
  • Blistered tomatoes: thread cherry tomatoes onto skewers and roll them over the coals until they split.
  • Quick chimichurri: parsley, garlic, red wine vinegar, oregano, chili flakes and olive oil. Make it while the coals light.

Troubleshooting

The crust will not darken. The surface was too wet or the fire was not hot enough. Next time dry brine longer and pat the steak dry before the sear. Make sure your coals are fully lit and close to the grate.

The center is uneven. You probably did not flip and rotate during the slow phase, or the steak was not uniformly thick. Butterflying or tying thin edges helps with oddly shaped cuts.

It overshot. The slow phase can creep faster than you expect near the end. Start checking temperature earlier and pull five degrees sooner next time.

It tastes bitter. Old ash or a dirty grate can leave off flavors. Brush the grate clean while it is hot and let the coals settle before you start.

Scaling up for a crowd

To cook four or five big steaks, use the Full Size and stagger them on the cool side in a single row. Because the slow phase is forgiving, you can pull each steak as it hits temperature and park it on a sheet pan. When everything is ready, sear them two or three at a time. The steaks hold for 15 to 20 minutes before searing without any loss in quality, which is a gift when you are also juggling sides and guests.

Another trick for a crowd: buy one enormous 3-inch steak and treat it like a small roast. Reverse sear it, slice it on a board, and let people take pieces. It is dramatic, easier to manage than six separate steaks, and every slice is perfectly cooked.

A word on charcoal

Lump charcoal lights quickly and burns hot, which is great for the sear but means the slow phase may need a refill on a long cook. Briquettes burn steadier and longer. Either works. Whatever you choose, never use lighter fluid; it leaves a chemical taste, and a chimney starter makes it unnecessary.

The takeaway

The reverse sear is less a recipe than a way of thinking: separate cooking from browning, and give each the conditions it needs. Once you have done it on a two-zone charcoal fire, it is hard to go back. The method works for thick pork chops, lamb loin chops, and even a pork tenderloin. Build the fire, trust the thermometer, and give the steak a few quiet minutes before the drama at the end.

Quick questions

Can I reverse sear a thin steak?

Not really. Steaks under about 1.25 inches overcook during the sear. Grill thin steaks hot and fast instead.

Do I need a lid to reverse sear?

A lid speeds the slow phase but is not required. On an open grill the cool side cooks more slowly, so allow extra time and flip and rotate more often.

How long should a reverse-seared steak rest?

About five minutes. The gentle start means very little carryover cooking and minimal juice loss.
