The Home Cook's Meat Temperature Guide

Safe minimums, chef's preferences, carryover cooking and how to use an instant-read thermometer so you never serve raw chicken or dry pork again.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

Every experienced cook will tell you the same thing: the single cheapest way to improve your grilling is to buy an instant-read thermometer and use it every time. Color, firmness, cooking time and the poke test are all unreliable. Meat looks different depending on the light, its age and how it was raised. Fires burn hotter or cooler depending on the weather. A thermometer cuts through all of that and tells you exactly what is happening inside your food.

This guide covers safe minimum temperatures, the temperatures many cooks actually prefer, how carryover cooking works, and how to take an accurate reading.

Safe minimum temperatures

These are the USDA's recommended safe minimum internal temperatures. They are designed to kill harmful bacteria.

Doneness for steaks and roasts

Whole cuts of beef and lamb have bacteria mostly on the surface, which is why many people choose to eat them below 145°F. The temperatures below are common restaurant-style targets. Choosing to eat meat cooked to these levels is a personal decision.

Pork

Modern pork is very lean and dries out quickly when overcooked. At USDA's 145°F with a 3-minute rest, pork chops and tenderloin are juicy with a blush of pink. Tough cuts like shoulder and ribs are best cooked much higher, to 195 to 203°F, until collagen has melted and the meat becomes tender.

Chicken and turkey

All poultry must reach 165°F to be considered safe. However:

  • Breasts are at their best right at 160 to 165°F. Pull them at about 160°F and let carryover take them to 165°F.
  • Thighs and legs have more connective tissue and taste better at 175 to 190°F, when that tissue has softened.

Fish

USDA recommends 145°F, at which fish is opaque and flakes easily. Many cooks prefer salmon at 120 to 125°F and tuna seared rare. Use high-quality, very fresh fish if you choose lower temperatures.

Carryover cooking

Food continues to cook after you remove it from the heat. The hot outer layers keep pushing heat toward the center. The amount of carryover depends on the size of the food and how hot the cooking environment was:

  • Thin steaks and chops: 2 to 3°F
  • Thick steaks: 3 to 7°F
  • Roasts and whole chickens: 5 to 10°F
  • Reverse-seared steaks: very little, about 2 to 4°F

Pull food from the grill a few degrees below your target and let it rest.

How to use an instant-read thermometer

Where to insert

  • Insert the probe into the thickest part of the food.
  • For steaks and chops, insert from the side, horizontally, so the tip sits in the center.
  • Avoid touching bone, which conducts heat and gives a false high reading.
  • For whole chickens, check both the thickest part of the breast and the thigh, near but not touching the bone.
  • For burgers, insert from the side into the center.

How to read

Wait for the reading to stabilize, usually 2 to 4 seconds with a good instant-read thermometer. Then move the probe a little deeper or shallower. The lowest reading you find is the true center temperature.

Check more than one piece

Food at the edge of the fire cooks differently from food in the middle. Check each piece, or at least the largest ones.

Choosing a thermometer

A good instant-read thermometer should be fast, accurate, easy to read and easy to clean. Features worth looking for:

  • Speed: readings in 2 to 4 seconds keep your hand away from the heat and prevent heat loss.
  • Accuracy: within about ±2°F.
  • Range: wide enough for everything from frozen to deep-frying temperatures.
  • Backlight: for evening cooks.
  • Folding probe: for safe storage.
  • Magnet or hang hole: so it is always at hand.

Calibrating

Check a thermometer's accuracy occasionally with an ice bath: fill a glass with ice, add cold water, stir and wait a minute. Insert the probe without touching the glass. It should read 32°F. Some thermometers can be recalibrated; consult the instructions.

Temperature and texture: a deeper look

Why does meat change so dramatically within a few degrees?

  • Around 120°F: proteins called myosin begin to set. Meat starts to firm up and turn opaque.
  • Around 140°F: collagen begins to shrink and squeeze out juices. Meat turns pinker-gray.
  • Around 150 to 160°F: another protein, actin, sets. Meat becomes much firmer and dries out noticeably.
  • Above 160°F: collagen slowly converts to gelatin over time. This is why tough cuts become tender after long, slow cooking at high temperatures.

Understanding this explains why a lean steak should be cooked to medium-rare, while a pork shoulder should be cooked until it is almost falling apart.

Resting meat

Resting lets temperature equalize and lets the juices redistribute slightly. Thin cuts need only a couple of minutes. Steaks need about 5 minutes. Roasts and whole chickens need 10 to 20 minutes. Rest meat on a board or warm plate, uncovered or loosely tented with foil, to avoid steaming the crust.

Hygiene with thermometers

Clean the probe with hot, soapy water or an alcohol wipe between readings when checking raw meat and cooked meat. Never put a thermometer that touched raw poultry into cooked food without cleaning it first.

Quick cheat sheet

  • Steak, medium-rare: pull at 125 to 130°F
  • Pork chops and tenderloin: pull at 140 to 145°F
  • Chicken breast: pull at 160°F
  • Chicken thighs: pull at 175 to 185°F
  • Burgers: 160°F
  • Salmon: 125°F (preference) to 145°F (USDA)
  • Shrimp: opaque, about 145°F
  • Pork shoulder for pulling: 195 to 203°F

The takeaway

Stop guessing and start measuring. A reliable instant-read thermometer turns every cook from a hopeful experiment into a predictable success. Learn the safe minimums, decide on your preferred doneness, account for carryover, and enjoy perfectly cooked food every time.

Going further: using a thermometer like a pro

Where to probe

Insert the probe into the thickest part of the meat, from the side when possible, so the tip sits in the center. Avoid touching bone, which conducts heat and gives a false reading. For burgers, probe from the side into the middle of the patty. For whole poultry, check the thickest part of the thigh and the breast.

Check in more than one spot

Large or irregular cuts cook unevenly. Probe two or three places and go by the lowest reading.

Carryover cooking

Thick cuts keep cooking after they leave the grill as heat moves from the hot exterior to the center. A thick steak can rise 3 to 7°F while resting. Pull it a few degrees below your target. Thin cuts and fish rise less.

Fast reads mean less heat lost

A fast thermometer means less time standing over the coals with the lid off and less time for the food to cool. A three-second-or-so answer, like the one from the Slot Grill thermometer, makes checking easy.

Checking accuracy

Test your thermometer occasionally in a glass packed with ice and a little water, stirred well. It should read close to 32°F.

Keep it clean

Wipe the probe after each use and between raw and cooked foods. For thermometers that are rinse-safe but not submersible, rinse the probe only and keep the body out of the water.

Cook's notebook

Key numbers

Poultry 165°F, ground meats 160°F, whole cuts of pork and beef 145°F with a 3-minute rest.

Quick questions

What temperature should chicken reach?

USDA's safe minimum for all poultry is 165°F. Thighs taste best around 175 to 185°F.

Where should I insert a meat thermometer?

Into the thickest part of the food, avoiding bone. For steaks, insert horizontally from the side.

What is carryover cooking?

Food keeps getting hotter inside after it leaves the heat, usually 2 to 10°F depending on size.
