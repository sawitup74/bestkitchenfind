Crispy-Skin Grilled Salmon That Never Sticks

Salmon is the fish most people fear grilling. Here is the dry-skin, hot-steel method that releases cleanly every time, plus three sauces.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

Ask a room full of home cooks which food they are most afraid to put on a grill and fish will win in a landslide. Everyone has a story about a beautiful fillet that welded itself to the grate and came off in shreds. Salmon is actually one of the easiest fish to grill well, as long as you understand why fish sticks and how to stop it. This method gives you shatteringly crisp skin, a juicy, barely translucent center and a fillet that lifts off the steel in one piece.

Why fish sticks

Proteins bond to hot metal. When raw fish touches a grate, the surface proteins grab onto microscopic pits in the steel. As the fish cooks and its surface browns and dries, those bonds release on their own. The sticking disaster happens when you try to move the fillet before that release, or when the grate is not hot or clean enough for the release to happen at all.

Three things fix it:

  1. A screaming hot, spotless grate. Heat makes the proteins set and brown quickly. Old, carbonized food gives fresh proteins something to cling to.
  2. A dry fish surface. Moisture creates steam, which prevents browning and keeps the proteins in a sticky state.
  3. Patience. Do not touch the fish until it releases naturally.

A well-seasoned carbon steel grate is a big advantage here. The polymerized oil layer on seasoned steel acts like a natural non-stick coating, much like a seasoned cast-iron pan. If your grate is freshly seasoned and you keep it that way, fish becomes dramatically easier.

Choosing salmon

For grilling, buy skin-on fillets about 1 to 1.5 inches thick at the center. The skin protects the delicate flesh from the fire and turns into the best part of the dish. Center-cut portions of about 6 ounces each are ideal because they are uniformly thick.

Wild salmon (king, sockeye, coho) is leaner and cooks faster; farmed Atlantic salmon has more fat and is more forgiving. King salmon is the richest wild variety. Sockeye has a deep red color and a bold flavor but very little fat, so pull it a little earlier.

Ask the fishmonger to remove pin bones, or run your fingers along the fillet and pull them out with tweezers or needle-nose pliers.

Prep: dry the skin

This is the step that makes the biggest difference.

  1. Pat the fillets completely dry with paper towels, especially the skin.
  2. Lay them skin-side up on a plate and refrigerate uncovered for 30 minutes to an hour. The cold, circulating air dries the skin further.
  3. Right before grilling, run the back of a knife firmly along the skin, scraping toward the edge. You will see moisture squeeze out. Wipe it away.
  4. Brush the skin side lightly with oil and season the flesh side with salt and pepper. Season the skin with salt only.

The fire

Salmon wants intense, direct heat. Light a full chimney of charcoal and pour it into an even layer under about two-thirds of the grill. Let the grate heat for a full 10 minutes; a hot grate is your best defense against sticking.

Clean the grate thoroughly with a stiff brush or scraper. Fold a paper towel into a pad, dip it in high-smoke-point oil, and wipe it over the grate with tongs. Repeat three or four times until the grate looks glossy.

Grilling

  1. Place the fillets skin-side down over the hottest part of the fire, diagonal to the bars.
  2. Do not touch them. Cook for 4 to 6 minutes. You will see the flesh turn opaque from the bottom up, creeping about two-thirds of the way up the side.
  3. Slide a thin, flexible metal spatula under one corner. If the skin releases easily, it is ready. If it resists, wait another 30 seconds and try again.
  4. Flip the fillets onto the flesh side for 30 seconds to 2 minutes, just long enough to mark them and finish cooking.
  5. Pull when the thickest part reaches about 125°F for moist, slightly translucent salmon, or up to 145°F (USDA's recommendation) for fully opaque, firmer fish.

Many cooks prefer to skip the flip entirely: cook skin-side down until the top is nearly opaque, then slide the spatula between the skin and the flesh. The fillet lifts off cleanly and the crisp skin stays on the grate for you to peel off and enjoy separately.

The griddle alternative

If you are nervous, or cooking delicate fish like flounder or tilapia, use a griddle plate. A very hot, well-oiled flat steel surface gives you perfect crisp skin with zero risk of falling through the grate. Sauce one: lemon-dill yogurt

  • 3/4 cup Greek yogurt
  • 2 tablespoons chopped fresh dill
  • Zest and juice of half a lemon
  • 1 small garlic clove, grated
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • Salt and pepper

Stir together and chill. Cool, tangy and perfect for summer.

Sauce two: miso-honey glaze

  • 2 tablespoons white miso
  • 1 tablespoon honey
  • 1 tablespoon rice vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon soy sauce
  • 1 teaspoon grated ginger
  • 1 teaspoon toasted sesame oil

Whisk until smooth. Brush on the flesh side after flipping and cook just until it bubbles, 30 to 60 seconds. Sugar burns fast, so watch it.

Sauce three: charred tomato salsa

While the coals heat, grill 4 plum tomatoes, a jalapeño and half a red onion until blistered. Chop finely and toss with lime juice, cilantro, salt and a splash of olive oil. Spoon over the salmon right before serving.

Cedar planks and other options

Plank grilling gives salmon a gentle, smoky, aromatic finish. Soak an untreated cedar plank in water for at least an hour, set the fillet on top skin-side down, and place the plank over medium indirect heat. The fish cooks in 15 to 25 minutes with no sticking risk and no flip. Keep a spray bottle of water nearby in case the plank edges catch.

Foil packets also work for very delicate fish: layer fillets on foil with lemon slices, herbs, butter and a splash of white wine, seal tightly, and grill over medium heat for 10 to 12 minutes. You will not get crisp skin, but you will get moist, fragrant fish.

Whole side of salmon for a crowd

A whole side of salmon, about 2.5 to 3 pounds, feeds six to eight and makes a stunning centerpiece. Grilling it directly on the grate is risky because it is hard to flip. Instead, lay it on a large sheet of heavy-duty foil or a long plank, skin-side down, and cook over medium indirect heat with the coals banked to one side. It takes 20 to 30 minutes. Troubleshooting

Skin stuck. The grate was not hot or clean enough, the skin was wet, or you moved it too soon. Address all three.

Flesh fell apart. Thin parts of a fillet are fragile. Use center-cut portions and a wide, thin spatula.

White stuff oozing out. That is albumin, a protein pushed out as the muscle contracts. A little is normal; a lot means the fish cooked too hot or too long. Brining for 10 minutes in a salt solution (1 tablespoon salt per cup of water) reduces it.

Fishy taste. The salmon was not fresh. Buy from a trusted counter or use well-sealed frozen fillets.

Storing and reusing leftovers

Grilled salmon keeps for two to three days. Flake it into pasta with peas and lemon cream, mix into scrambled eggs with chives, or make salmon cakes with breadcrumbs, mustard and herbs, then fry them on a griddle until golden.

The takeaway

Hot grate, dry skin, hands off. Those three rules turn the scariest food on the grill into one of the simplest. Once you nail crispy skin, you will start grilling salmon every week.

Quick questions

Why does salmon stick to the grill?

Proteins bond to hot metal. A very hot, clean, oiled grate and a dry fish surface let the fillet release on its own once browned.

Should I grill salmon skin-side down first?

Yes. The skin protects the flesh, crisps nicely, and makes the fillet easy to move.

What temperature is grilled salmon done?

USDA recommends 145°F; many cooks prefer pulling at about 125°F for a moist, slightly translucent center.
