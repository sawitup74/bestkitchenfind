Carbon Steel Care

Rust Rescue: Bringing Carbon Steel Back to Life

Found orange spots on your grill or griddle? Here is how to assess the damage, remove rust safely and re-season so it never comes back.

7 min read · Best Kitchen Find test kitchen

It happens to everyone. You left the grill out in a rainstorm, forgot to dry the griddle, or stored it in a damp garage over the winter. Now there is rust on your carbon steel. The good news: rust on carbon steel is almost always cosmetic and completely fixable. Steel is tough. With some scrubbing and a fresh round of seasoning, it will be back to cooking in an afternoon.

Why carbon steel rusts

Carbon steel is mostly iron. When iron is exposed to moisture and oxygen, it oxidizes, forming iron oxide: rust. Seasoning protects steel by creating a barrier between the metal and the air. When that barrier is thin, damaged or missing, rust can form, sometimes within hours if the steel is wet.

Common causes:

  • Leaving steel wet after cleaning
  • Rain, dew or humidity during storage
  • Ash left in the firebox absorbing moisture
  • Acidic foods wearing down seasoning
  • Storing steel in a sealed container with trapped moisture

Assessing the damage

Most rust falls into one of three categories:

Light surface rust

A thin orange haze or a few small spots. It wipes away with a paper towel and some oil, or a quick scrub. This is the most common type and the easiest to fix.

Moderate rust

Larger patches, rough texture, perhaps some flaking. Needs scrubbing with steel wool or a stiff brush and re-seasoning.

Heavy rust

Thick, flaky, scaly rust across large areas, possibly with pitting. Requires more aggressive removal. Even heavy rust is usually fixable on thick steel. Thin, stamped metal that has rusted through is another matter, but thick plates rarely have that problem.

What you will need

  • Steel wool or a stiff scrubbing pad
  • A stiff brush
  • White vinegar (for moderate to heavy rust)
  • Baking soda
  • Dish soap
  • Paper towels or rags
  • Oil or seasoning balm
  • Rubber gloves
  • An oven or a charcoal fire

Fixing light surface rust

  1. Scrub the rusty areas with steel wool or a stiff pad and a little oil until the orange is gone and you see bare gray or dark steel.
  2. Wipe clean with a paper towel.
  3. Wash with warm soapy water, rinse and dry immediately.
  4. Heat the steel to drive off moisture.
  5. Wipe on the faintest film of oil or balm and heat it until it smokes and darkens.

Done. Light rust often comes off in a few minutes.

Fixing moderate rust

  1. Scrub with dry steel wool or a stiff wire brush to remove loose rust.
  2. Wash with warm soapy water.
  3. For stubborn rust, make a paste of baking soda and water and scrub it into the rusty area with steel wool.
  4. Rinse, dry thoroughly and heat to remove moisture.
  5. Season with 2 to 3 thin coats.

Fixing heavy rust with vinegar

Vinegar is a mild acid that dissolves rust. It also dissolves seasoning and, if left too long, can etch the steel itself. Use it carefully.

  1. Mix equal parts white vinegar and water.
  2. Soak or wipe the rusty areas with the solution. For flat plates, you can lay them in a shallow tray or wrap them in vinegar-soaked rags.
  3. Check every 30 minutes. Most rust will loosen within 1 to 2 hours. Do not leave steel in vinegar overnight.
  4. Scrub off the loosened rust with steel wool.
  5. Rinse thoroughly with water.
  6. Neutralize any remaining acid by scrubbing with a paste of baking soda and water. Rinse again.
  7. Dry immediately and heat to drive off all moisture. Bare steel can flash rust within minutes after the vinegar treatment, so work quickly.
  8. Season with 3 to 4 thin coats.

Re-seasoning after rust removal

Rust removal leaves bare steel with little or no seasoning. Treat it like new steel:

  1. Make sure it is completely clean and dry.
  2. Apply the thinnest possible coat of oil or seasoning balm.
  3. Wipe off as much as you can.
  4. Bake at 400 to 450°F for an hour, or heat over a charcoal fire until it smokes and darkens.
  5. Let it cool and repeat 2 to 4 times.

Slot Grill Seasoning Balm, for instance, is directed to be applied in a very thin coat and baked at 400°F for one hour, repeated as needed to build the coating.

Pitting

Deep rust can leave small pits in the steel surface. Pitting is cosmetic on thick steel and does not affect cooking much. Seasoning will fill in small pits over time.

Preventing rust

After every cook

  • Scrape and wipe the grate or griddle while warm.
  • Apply a very thin coat of oil.
  • Empty the ash once it is cold.

Storage

  • Store indoors in a dry place whenever possible: a closet, pantry, garage shelf or basement.
  • Flat-pack grills are especially easy to store indoors. Broken down, a Compact is roughly an inch deep and a Full Size roughly an inch and a quarter, which means a closet shelf, the space beneath a bed or a car trunk all work.
  • Avoid sealed plastic bins, which trap humidity. A cloth bag or open shelf is better.
  • If you must store outside, use a breathable, waterproof cover and keep the grill off the ground.

Seasonal prep

Before storing for the winter or a long break:

  1. Deep clean the grill.
  2. Dry completely.
  3. Apply a slightly more generous (but still thin) coat of seasoning to all surfaces, inside and out.
  4. Wrap in newspaper or a cloth to absorb moisture.
  5. Store somewhere dry.

Coastal and humid environments

Salty air near the ocean is especially hard on steel. If you live or cook near the coast:

  • Rinse off salt spray with fresh water after use.
  • Dry and oil more frequently.
  • Store indoors.

Rust and food safety

A small amount of surface rust is not considered harmful, but rust can give food a metallic taste and causes sticking. Always remove rust from cooking surfaces before cooking.

When to call it

Thick carbon steel almost never needs to be thrown away because of rust. If a piece has rusted completely through or has severe structural damage, that is another story, but that is extremely rare with heavy-gauge steel.

The takeaway

Rust is not the end of the world for carbon steel; it is a normal part of owning it. Scrub it off, dry it well, season it again, and build better habits. Clean, dry and oil after every cook, store it indoors and empty the ash. Your steel will last a lifetime.

Going further: preventing rust in the first place

Humidity is the enemy

Rust needs moisture and oxygen. A grill stored in a damp garage or covered by a tarp that traps condensation will rust faster than one stored indoors. In humid climates, store the grill inside if you can, or in a breathable bag with a couple of silica gel packets.

The post-cook oil wipe

The single best rust prevention habit is a thin oil wipe after every cook while the steel is still warm. The warmth helps the oil flow into the surface and begins to polymerize it.

Rain and dew

If your grill sits outside and gets rained on, dry it as soon as you can. Light a small fire to drive off moisture, then oil it. Morning dew can do the same damage over time.

Travel tips

After a camping or beach trip, unpack the grill as soon as you get home. Salt air and damp gear are hard on steel. Wipe off sand and salt, dry, and oil.

When to call it surface rust

A light orange haze that wipes away with a paper towel and oil is surface rust and needs no more than a scrub and a fresh coat. Deeper flaking or pitting calls for the full rescue: scrub to bare steel, dry, and rebuild several thin layers of seasoning.

Don't panic

Carbon steel is forgiving. Rust looks alarming but is almost always fixable with some elbow grease and a few rounds of seasoning.

Quick questions

Is rust on my grill dangerous?

A small amount of surface rust is not considered harmful, but remove it before cooking for flavor and performance.

How do I remove heavy rust from carbon steel?

Soak in a 50/50 vinegar and water mix for 1 to 2 hours, scrub, neutralize with baking soda, dry and re-season.

How do I prevent rust in storage?

Store the grill indoors and dry, with a thin coat of seasoning, avoiding sealed bins that trap humidity.
